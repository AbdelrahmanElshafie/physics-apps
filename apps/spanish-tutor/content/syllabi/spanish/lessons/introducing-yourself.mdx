title: Saying who you are
summary: Me llamo, soy, and soy de — names, nationality, and the first appearance of ser.
readingMinutes: 20
---

Greetings got you into a conversation. This lesson gets you through the next thirty seconds of
it: your name, where you're from, what you do. It also introduces **ser**, the first of Spanish's
two verbs for "to be" — and one habit English speakers have to unlearn in the same breath.

## 1. Your name: me llamo

<VocabCard word="me llamo" meaning="my name is — literally 'I call myself'" pronunciation="me YA-mo — ll is a y sound" />

<Dialogue
  caption="Exchanging names"
  lines={[
    { speaker: 'A', text: '¿Cómo te llamas?', meaning: "What's your name? (literally: how do you call yourself?)" },
    { speaker: 'B', text: 'Me llamo Ana. ¿Y tú?', meaning: "My name is Ana. And you?" },
    { speaker: 'A', text: 'Me llamo Pablo.', meaning: 'My name is Pablo.' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="Formal version: ¿Cómo se llama usted?">
To a stranger, someone older, or anyone you'd address formally, the question is ¿Cómo se llama
usted? The tú/usted distinction gets its own lesson in Phase 3; for now, know that both forms
exist and that te llamas is the one between equals.
</Callout>

## 2. Soy — the first appearance of ser

<VocabCard word="soy" meaning="I am — from ser, 'to be'" />

soy covers three of the first things you'll want to say about yourself:

<Compare
  columns={['Pattern', 'Example', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['soy + name', 'Soy Ana.', 'I am Ana.'],
    ['soy + profession', 'Soy estudiante.', 'I am a student.'],
    ['soy + nationality', 'Soy inglesa.', "I am English. (a woman speaking)"],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1]}
  caption="Three jobs, one verb form. soy never changes — only what follows it does."
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="No article before a profession">
English says "I am **a** student". Spanish says Soy estudiante — no un, no una. The same goes for
every profession: soy profesora, soy médico. Putting the article in (soy un estudiante) is
understood, but it's the most reliable early marker of an English speaker, and it's one word
easier to leave out than to put in.
</Callout>

<VocabCard word="estudiante" meaning="student — one form for both genders" />
<VocabCard word="profesor" meaning="teacher (male)" />
<VocabCard word="profesora" meaning="teacher (female)" />

<Predict
  question="How do you say 'I am a student'?"
  options={['Soy un estudiante', 'Soy estudiante', 'Estoy estudiante', 'Soy el estudiante']}
  answer={1}
>
  Soy estudiante — no article. (Estoy is the *other* "to be", estar, which is never used for what
  someone is; that distinction is the whole of Phase 3.)
</Predict>

## 4. Where you're from: soy de

<VocabCard word="¿De dónde eres?" meaning="Where are you from? — literally 'from where are you?'" note="dónde carries its accent because it's a question word — every question word does, as Phase 5 will make official." />

<GrammarPoint pattern="Soy de Londres." title="de = from">

Soy de Londres — "I am from London." de is "from" (and also "of" — it'll do a lot of work later).
Put the place after it and the sentence is done.

</GrammarPoint>

<Dialogue
  caption="Putting it together"
  lines={[
    { speaker: 'A', text: '¿De dónde eres?', meaning: 'Where are you from?' },
    { speaker: 'B', text: 'Soy de Madrid. Soy española. ¿Y tú?', meaning: "I'm from Madrid. I'm Spanish. And you?" },
    { speaker: 'A', text: 'Soy de Londres. Soy estudiante.', meaning: "I'm from London. I'm a student." },
  ]}
/>

## 5. The rest of ser — a preview

soy and eres are two of six forms. Here is the whole verb, with the pronouns it goes with, so the
shape is familiar before Phase 3 and Phase 4 return to it properly:
