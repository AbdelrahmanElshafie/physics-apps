You have been using the forms of ser and estar next to a column of words — yo, tú, él — without
those words being explained. This lesson explains them: the six subject pronouns, the reason Spanish
drops them most of the time, and the one choice English never asks you to make — whether the
person in front of you is **tú** or **usted**.

## 1. The six persons

/>

<Callout kind="note" title="Gender in the plurals">
nosotros, vosotros, ellos are the default. The -as forms are for a group made up entirely of
women; one man in the group and it goes back to -os. There is no neutral form — the masculine
does double duty, exactly as it does for mixed groups of nouns in the plurals lesson.
</Callout>

## 2. Why the pronoun is usually missing

<GrammarPoint pattern="Soy de Madrid." title="The ending already says who" speak="Soy de Madrid.">

soy can only mean "I am". hablo can only mean "I speak". The ending carries the subject, so saying
yo as well is like saying "I, me, I am" — not wrong, just loud. The neutral Spanish sentence drops
the pronoun: Soy de Madrid, not Yo soy de Madrid.

</GrammarPoint>

You keep the pronoun in three situations:

<Compare
  columns={['When', 'Example', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['contrast', 'Yo soy de Londres, pero ella es de Madrid.', "I'm from London, but she's from Madrid."],
    ['clearing up él / ella / usted', 'Ella es profesora.', "She's a teacher. (es alone could be he, she, or you)"],
    ['bouncing a question back', '¿Y tú?', 'And you?'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1]}
  caption="Contrast is the big one. If you would stress the pronoun in English — 'I'M from London, SHE'S from Madrid' — Spanish says it out loud."
/>

<Predict
  question="Which is the neutral, everyday way to say 'I am a student'?"
  options={['Yo soy estudiante.', 'Soy estudiante.', 'Soy yo estudiante.']}
  answer={1}
>
  Soy estudiante. The yo version isn't wrong — it's what you'd say if someone had just claimed you
  were a teacher — but as a plain statement it sounds emphatic.
</Predict>

## 3. Tú or usted?

<Compare
  columns={['tú', 'usted']}
  rows={[
    ['friends, family, classmates, children', 'strangers, older people, officials, customers'],
    ['anyone who calls you tú', 'anyone who calls you usted'],
    ['takes the tú verb form — eres, estás', 'takes the él / ella verb form — es, está'],
  ]}
  caption="When in doubt with an adult you don't know, start with usted. Being offered tú — 'puedes tutearme', you can call me tú — is a small, pleasant moment, and it happens quickly in Spain."
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="Why usted uses the he/she form">
usted is a worn-down vuestra merced, "your grace" — you were talking *about* someone's grace
rather than *to* them, so the verb went into the third person. The history is gone; the grammar
stayed. ¿Cómo está usted? uses the same está as ¿Cómo está ella?
</Callout>
