---
title: Gender and articles
summary: Every noun is masculine or feminine, the ending usually gives it away, and the article has to match.
readingMinutes: 20
---

English nouns don't have gender — "table" isn't masculine or feminine, it's just a table. Every
Spanish noun is one or the other. This has nothing to do with the thing itself: a dress (el
vestido) is masculine and a tie (la corbata) is feminine. It is a grammatical category, and it
matters because several other words in a sentence have to change to match it — starting, in this
lesson, with "the" and "a".

## 2. The ending usually tells you

For most nouns, the last letter is the answer:

<Compare
  columns={['Ending', 'Gender', 'Examples']}
  rows={[
    ['-o', 'masculine', 'el libro · el coche · el vestido'],
    ['-a', 'feminine', 'la casa · la mesa · la silla'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[2]}
  caption="This covers the large majority of nouns you will meet. The endings below cover most of the rest."
/>

<Predict
  question="mesa (table) ends in -a. Which article goes with it?"
  options={['el mesa', 'la mesa', 'Either, depending on the speaker', 'un mesa']}
  answer={1}
>
  la mesa — -a is the feminine ending, so the feminine article. (And "a table" would be una mesa.)
</Predict>

## 3. Endings that are reliable even though they aren't -o or -a

<Compare
  columns={['Ending', 'Gender', 'Examples']}

    ['-dad, -tad', 'feminine', 'la ciudad · la universidad · la libertad'],

/>

<VocabCard word="la ciudad" meaning="the city (feminine — -dad)" />
<VocabCard word="el problema" meaning="the problem (masculine, despite the -a)" />

<Callout kind="warning" title="el problema is the exception worth memorising first">
Nouns in -ma are the trap: they look feminine and are masculine. problema, programa, sistema,
tema (topic), idioma (language), clima (climate) — all el. Learn the group, not the words one at a
time, and la problema will never come out of your mouth.
</Callout>

## 4. The famous exceptions

A small number of very common words break the -o/-a rule outright. There is no pattern to find;
they are simply learned with their article attached:

<Compare
  columns={['Word', 'Looks', 'Is', 'Meaning']}
  rows={[
    ['el día', 'feminine (-a)', 'masculine', 'day'],
    ['el mapa', 'feminine (-a)', 'masculine', 'map'],
    ['la mano', 'masculine (-o)', 'feminine', 'hand'],
    ['la foto', 'masculine (-o)', 'feminine', 'photo — short for la fotografía'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[0]}
  caption="Four words, all common enough that you'll meet them in the first weeks. Say them with the article every time — el día, la mano — and the gender comes free."
/>

<Predict
  question="mano ends in -o. What is 'the hand'?"
  options={['el mano', 'la mano', 'un mano', 'It has no article']}
  answer={1}
>
  la mano — one of the handful of true exceptions. The -o ending is the rule; mano is a word you
  learn with its article, because the rule gets it wrong.
</Predict>

## 5. Why this is the first agreement rule, not the only one

The article is just the first word that has to match a noun's gender. Adjectives do too (the next
lesson), and so do several pronouns later on. Get the gender of a noun wrong and the error spreads
to every word that agrees with it — which is why this comes before vocabulary, not after.

<GrammarPoint pattern="la casa  →  una casa  →  la casa blanca" title="The agreement chain, previewed">

Once casa is feminine, everything attached to it goes feminine: the article (la, una), and — next
lesson — the adjective (blanca, not blanco). One decision about the noun, propagated outward.

</GrammarPoint>
