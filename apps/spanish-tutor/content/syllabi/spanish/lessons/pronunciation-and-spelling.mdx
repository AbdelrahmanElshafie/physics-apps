title: Pronunciation and spelling

The single most useful fact about Spanish, and the reason it's a kinder language to start than
English or French: **spelling is pronunciation.** Once you know how each letter sounds, you can
say any written word correctly on first sight — and spell any word you hear. This lesson is the
code. Everything after it assumes you can read a word aloud without being told how.

## 1. Five vowels, five sounds, no exceptions

English has around a dozen vowel sounds and spells them chaotically ("though", "through",
"tough"). Spanish has five, and each letter makes exactly one sound, every time, in every word:

<Compare
  columns={['Letter', 'Sounds like', 'Example']}
  rows={[
    ['a', 'the a in "father" — never "cat", never "cake"', 'casa'],
    ['e', 'the e in "bet" — never "be"', 'mesa'],
    ['i', 'the ee in "see"', 'sí'],
    ['o', 'the o in "more" — never "go"', 'no'],
    ['u', 'the oo in "food"', 'tú'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[2]}
  caption="Short, crisp, and never reduced. An unstressed Spanish vowel keeps its full sound — there is no mumbled 'uh' the way English turns 'banana' into buh-NA-nuh."
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="The English habit to break first">
English speakers automatically lengthen and glide stressed vowels ("no" becomes "no-u", "say"
becomes "sa-y"). Spanish vowels don't glide. *No* is a short, clean "no", not "nowe". Listen for
it in the audio throughout this lesson — it is the single most audible marker of an English
accent, and it's fixable from day one.
</Callout>

## 2. Consonants that behave

Most consonants sound roughly as an English speaker would guess — b, d, f, k, l, m, n, p, s, t.
A handful don't, and they're worth drilling individually:

## 3. The letters that change with what follows

Two consonants have two sounds, decided entirely by the next vowel — the same split English has
in "cat" vs. "cent", but applied with total consistency:

<Compare
  columns={['Letter', 'Before a, o, u', 'Before e, i']}
  rows={[
    ['c', 'hard k — casa, como', 'th (Spain) / s (Latin America) — cerveza, gracias'],
    ['g', 'hard g — gato, gusto', 'like j, a hard h — gente, gigante'],
  ]}
  caption="To keep a hard k or g sound before e or i, Spanish writes qu or gu: que, quién, guerra. The u there is silent — it's a spelling device."
/>

<VocabCard word="gato" meaning="cat" pronunciation="GA-to — hard g" />
<VocabCard word="gente" meaning="people" pronunciation="HEN-te — g before e is like j" />

<Callout kind="note" title="Spain vs. Latin America, in one line">
The c-before-e/i and z sound is "th" in most of Spain and "s" across Latin America. Both are
correct; the audio in this course uses the Spain pronunciation. Pick whichever you'll use more and
don't mix them in one sentence. That is the whole of the regional difference you need right now.
</Callout>

## 4. r and rr — the one genuinely new sound

A single r between vowels is a quick tap of the tongue, like the tt in American "butter". A
double rr — or an r at the start of a word — is rolled: the tongue vibrates. The difference can
be the whole word:

<Compare
  columns={['Word', 'r sound', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['pero', 'single tap', 'but'],
    ['perro', 'rolled', 'dog'],
    ['caro', 'single tap', 'expensive'],
    ['carro', 'rolled', 'car (Latin America)'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[0]}
  caption="Play pero and perro back to back. If you can't roll an r yet, that's normal — it's a muscle habit that takes weeks, and a tapped r is still understood. Just don't collapse them into an English r."
/>

<Predict
  question="You say pero when you meant perro. What have you actually said?"
  options={["Dog, with an accent — people will understand", 'But — a completely different word', 'Nothing, it is not a word', 'Dog, but rudely']}
  answer={1}
>
  "But." The r/rr contrast is a real difference between real words, not a matter of accent — the
  same way "ship" and "sheep" are different words in English, however close they sound to a
  non-native ear.
</Predict>

## 5. ¿ and ¡ are part of the spelling

<GrammarPoint pattern="¿Cómo estás?  ·  ¡Hola!" title="Opening marks">

Questions and exclamations open with an upside-down mark as well as closing with the usual one.
This isn't optional or old-fashioned: a question written without its ¿ is a spelling error. The
opening mark is useful, too — it tells you to start the rising intonation before you reach the
end of the sentence.

</GrammarPoint>
