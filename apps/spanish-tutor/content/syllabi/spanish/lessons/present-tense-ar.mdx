title: "Present tense: -ar verbs"
summary: Hablar and the six endings that every regular -ar verb shares.
readingMinutes: 25

Everything so far has used two verbs, ser and estar, both irregular — you learned their forms as
lists. This lesson is the opposite experience: one pattern, six endings, and suddenly hundreds of
verbs are yours. Spanish verbs come in three families, named after the last two letters of their
dictionary form; the **-ar** family is the largest, and it is the one we start with.

## 1. The infinitive, and the stem

<VocabCard word="hablar" meaning="to speak, to talk" note="The dictionary form — the infinitive — always ends in -ar, -er or -ir. That ending is what tells you which family a verb belongs to." />

Take the -ar off and what is left is the **stem**: habl-. Every present-tense form is the stem plus
an ending, and the ending tells you who is doing it:

<Compare
  columns={['', 'hablar', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['yo', 'hablo', 'I speak'],
    ['tú', 'hablas', 'you speak'],
    ['él / ella / usted', 'habla', 'he / she speaks · you (formal) speak'],
    ['nosotros', 'hablamos', 'we speak'],
    ['vosotros', 'habláis', 'you (plural, Spain) speak'],
    ['ellos / ellas / ustedes', 'hablan', 'they speak · you (plural) speak'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1]}
  caption="-o, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an. Six endings, in the same six rows as ser and estar. This is the table to memorise; everything else in the lesson follows from it."
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="This is why the pronoun drops">
hablo can only be "I speak"; hablamos can only be "we speak". The ending does the pronoun's job,
which is exactly why the last lesson told you yo and nosotros are usually left out. The one
ambiguity is the third row — habla could be he, she, or usted — and that is the row where a
pronoun gets kept.
</Callout>

## 2. Same endings, any stem

Swap the stem and the endings don't move:

<Compare
  columns={['', 'trabajar — to work', 'estudiar — to study']}
  rows={[
    ['yo', 'trabajo', 'estudio'],
    ['tú', 'trabajas', 'estudias'],
    ['él / ella / usted', 'trabaja', 'estudia'],
    ['nosotros', 'trabajamos', 'estudiamos'],
    ['vosotros', 'trabajáis', 'estudiáis'],
    ['ellos / ellas / ustedes', 'trabajan', 'estudian'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1, 2]}
  caption="Three verbs, one set of endings. The pattern is completely regular: if you can conjugate hablar, you can conjugate every verb on this page."
/>

<Predict

  answer={2}
>
  trabajamos — stem trabaj- plus the nosotros ending -amos.
</Predict>

## 4. One Spanish present, three English ones

<GrammarPoint pattern="Hablo español." title="hablo = I speak, I am speaking, I do speak">

Spanish has no separate "I am speaking" in everyday use. hablo español covers "I speak Spanish"
(in general), "I am speaking Spanish" (right now), and "I do speak Spanish" (yes, really). Don't
reach for a second verb; the one form does all three.

</GrammarPoint>

## 6. The one accent in the table — and the one that isn't there yet

habláis is the only form in the present table with an accent (final-syllable stress, against rule
one). But the present yo form has a twin that will matter later:

<AccentDemo
  items={[
    { word: 'hablo', syllables: ['ha', 'blo'], stress: 0, meaning: 'I speak — present' },
    { word: 'habló', syllables: ['ha', 'bló'], stress: 1, meaning: 'he / she spoke — the preterite, coming in Phase 4' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="hablo and habló are different tenses">
Same letters, different stressed syllable, different meaning. The accent is the only thing on the
page that separates "I speak" from "he spoke" — one more reason this course checks accents on every
typed answer.
</Callout>
