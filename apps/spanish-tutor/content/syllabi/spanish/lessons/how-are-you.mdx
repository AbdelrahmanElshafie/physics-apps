You can say hello and say who you are. The next thing anyone says is "how are you?" — and in
Spanish that question uses a verb you haven't met yet. Not **ser**, the "to be" from the last
lesson, but **estar**: the *other* "to be". Why Spanish has two is the subject of the next lesson.
This one just gets the question and its answers into your mouth.

## 1. The question, twice

<Compare
  columns={['To a friend (tú)', 'To a stranger or elder (usted)', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['¿Cómo estás?', '¿Cómo está usted?', 'How are you?'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[0, 1]}
  caption="Two forms of one question. estás goes with tú, está with usted — the same split as te llamas / se llama."
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="The accent is doing real work">
estás and está both carry a written accent, and it isn't optional. esta without the accent is a
different word — "this" (esta casa, "this house"). You met está as a stress pair in the accents
lesson; this is where it starts appearing in sentences, and every typed exercise checks the mark.
</Callout>

## 3. Saying how you feel: estoy + adjective

<VocabCard word="estoy" meaning="I am — from estar" />

For anything more specific than bien or mal, you use **estoy** plus an adjective. And because it's
an adjective, it agrees with you — the rule from the plurals lesson, again:

<Compare
  columns={['A man says', 'A woman says', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['Estoy cansado.', 'Estoy cansada.', "I'm tired."],
    ['Estoy enfermo.', 'Estoy enferma.', "I'm ill."],
    ['Estoy contento.', 'Estoy contenta.', "I'm happy / pleased."],
    ['Estoy ocupado.', 'Estoy ocupada.', "I'm busy."],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[0, 1]}
  caption="bien never changes, but cansado does — one is an adverb, the other an adjective."
/>

<Predict
  question="A woman wants to say she's tired. Which is right?"
  options={['Estoy cansado', 'Estoy cansada', 'Soy cansada', 'Estoy bien cansado']}
  answer={1}
>
  Estoy cansada — estar for a state, and the adjective agrees with the speaker. Soy cansada would
  mean something closer to "I am a tiring person", and nobody wants to say that about themselves.
</Predict>

## 4. Why estoy and not soy?

<GrammarPoint pattern="Soy estudiante. Estoy cansada." title="Who you are vs. how you are">

Soy says what you *are* — your name, your job, your nationality. Estoy says how you *are* right
now — tired, ill, fine. English uses one verb for both; Spanish splits them, and the split is the
single most useful thing to get right early. The next lesson is about nothing else.

</GrammarPoint>

## 5. The rest of estar — a preview

Like ser, estar is irregular, so here is the whole thing to look at. Notice where the accents fall:
on every form except estoy and estamos.
