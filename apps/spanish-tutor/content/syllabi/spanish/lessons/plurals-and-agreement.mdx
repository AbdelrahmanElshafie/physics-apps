title: Plurals and adjective agreement
summary: Adjectives change to match their noun in both gender and number — the agreement system the rest of the course runs on.
readingMinutes: 20
---

The last lesson made one decision per noun — masculine or feminine — and showed it propagating
to the article. This lesson adds the second dimension, singular or plural, and the second
kind of word that has to agree: the adjective. Between them, gender and number account for most
of the "endings" an English speaker notices changing in Spanish, and they all follow from the
noun.

## 1. Making a noun plural

## 2. When the plural changes an accent

Adding -es adds a syllable, and that can move a word across the line between the two stress rules
from the accents lesson — so an accent appears or disappears, purely to keep the stress where it
was:

<Compare
  columns={['Singular', 'Plural', 'What happened']}
  rows={[
    ['nación (na-CIÓN)', 'naciones (na-CIO-nes)', 'ends in s now, so rule one puts stress on CIO by itself — the accent is no longer needed'],
    ['joven (JO-ven)', 'jóvenes (JÓ-ve-nes)', 'rule one would now say jo-VE-nes — the accent appears to keep the stress on JO'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[0, 1]}
  caption="The stress never moves. The accent comes and goes so that the rules still point at the right syllable — exactly what the accents lesson said it was for."
/>

<VocabCard word="los jóvenes" meaning="the young people — an accent appears in the plural" />

## 3. Adjectives agree in gender and number

An adjective ending in -o has four forms, one for each combination:

<Compare
  columns={['', 'Masculine', 'Feminine']}
  rows={[
    ['singular', 'blanco', 'blanca'],
    ['plural', 'blancos', 'blancas'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1, 2]}
  caption="el libro blanco · la casa blanca · los libros blancos · las casas blancas. One adjective, four spellings, all decided by the noun."
/>

<GrammarPoint pattern="la casa blanca" title="Noun first, then the adjective, agreeing">

Two things to notice. The adjective matches the noun — blanca, because casa is feminine singular.
And it comes *after* the noun, not before: Spanish says "the house white." That order is the
normal one, and the word-order lesson in Phase 5 covers the few adjectives that go in front.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="How do you say 'the white cars' (coche, masculine)?"
  options={['los coches blancos', 'los blancos coches', 'los coches blancas', 'las coches blancos']}
  answer={0}
>
  los coches blancos — masculine plural article, noun, then the adjective in its masculine plural
  form, after the noun. Every ending in the phrase is answering to coche.
</Predict>

## 4. Adjectives with only two forms

Adjectives that end in -e or a consonant don't change for gender — only for number:

<Compare
  columns={['Adjective', 'Singular (m & f)', 'Plural (m & f)']}
  rows={[
    ['grande — big', 'el libro grande · la casa grande', 'los libros grandes · las casas grandes'],
    ['azul — blue', 'el coche azul · la silla azul', 'los coches azules · las sillas azules'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1, 2]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="The exception: nationalities do take a feminine form">
Most consonant-ending adjectives have no feminine — but adjectives of nationality do. español →
española, inglés → inglesa, francés → francesa. And notice inglés → inglesa: the accent drops in
the feminine, because inglesa ends in a vowel and rule one now stresses GLE on its own. The
accents lesson keeps paying off.
</Callout>

<VocabCard word="inglés" meaning="English (masculine) — in-GLÉS, accent needed" />

## 5. One more rule: mixed groups are masculine

A group containing both masculine and feminine nouns takes the masculine plural: los libros y las
casas son **blancos**. One masculine member is enough.

<Callout kind="why" title="Why this is the system, not a list of endings">
Everything in this lesson follows from two decisions about the noun — its gender, and whether it's
plural. The article agrees, the adjective agrees, and in later lessons pronouns and past
participles agree too. There is nothing to decide at the adjective; it simply reports what the
noun already settled. Once that feels automatic, the endings stop being memorisation and become
bookkeeping.
</Callout>
