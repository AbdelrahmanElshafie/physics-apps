---
title: Ser vs. estar
summary: What something is versus how or where it is — the distinction English speakers get wrong for longest, and the adjectives whose meaning flips with it.
readingMinutes: 25
---

English has one verb "to be". Spanish has two, and it never lets you off choosing. You have met
both already — **soy estudiante**, **estoy cansada** — and the last lesson gave the rule in one
line: ser for who or what you are, estar for how or where you are. This lesson is that line taken
seriously, because it is the mistake English speakers keep making longest, and because a handful
of adjectives change meaning depending on which verb you put in front of them.

## 2. Ser: what something *is*

Ser classifies. It answers "what is it?", "who is it?", "what kind of thing is it?" — and it
covers the things that define a person or object rather than describe their current state.

<Compare
  columns={['Use', 'Example', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['identity', 'Es Ana.', "It's Ana."],
    ['profession', 'Ana es profesora.', 'Ana is a teacher.'],
    ['nationality / origin', 'Es de Madrid.', "She's from Madrid."],
    ['characteristic', 'Es alta y simpática.', "She's tall and nice."],
    ['material', 'La mesa es de madera.', 'The table is made of wood.'],
    ['time and date', 'Hoy es lunes.', 'Today is Monday.'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1]}
/>

<VocabCard word="hoy" meaning="today" />
<VocabCard word="simpático" meaning="nice, friendly (of a person) — not 'sympathetic'" pronunciation="sim-PA-ti-co" />

## 3. Estar: how or where something is

Estar describes. It answers "how is it right now?" and "where is it?" — the state something is in,
and the place it's in.

<Compare
  columns={['Use', 'Example', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['location', 'Madrid está en España.', 'Madrid is in Spain.'],
    ['location', 'El libro está en la mesa.', 'The book is on the table.'],
    ['location', 'Ana está en casa.', 'Ana is at home.'],
    ['state / condition', 'Estoy cansado.', "I'm tired."],
    ['feeling', 'Están contentos.', "They're happy."],
    ['result of a change', 'La puerta está abierta.', 'The door is open.'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1]}
  caption="Location of a thing or a person is always estar — even for Madrid, which isn't going anywhere."
/>

<VocabCard word="en casa" meaning="at home — no article, like 'at home' in English" />
<VocabCard word="¿Dónde está…?" meaning="Where is…? — the location question always takes estar" />

<Predict
  question="Which is right: 'Madrid ___ en España'?"
  options={['es', 'está']}
  answer={1}
>
  Madrid está en España. Where something is — even something permanent, like a city — is estar.
  This is the first place the "permanent vs. temporary" shortcut fails, which is why this course
  doesn't teach that shortcut.
</Predict>

## 4. The myth of permanent vs. temporary

Most beginners are told "ser is permanent, estar is temporary". It is a useful first guess and a
bad rule, and it breaks on the most ordinary sentences:

<Compare
  columns={['Spanish', 'Means', 'Why it breaks the shortcut']}
  rows={[
    ['Está muerto.', "He's dead.", 'as permanent as it gets — but a state, so estar'],
    ['Soy joven.', "I'm young.", "won't last — but a characteristic that classifies me, so ser"],
    ['Madrid está en España.', 'Madrid is in Spain.', "permanent location, still estar — location is estar, full stop"],
    ['Son las tres.', "It's three o'clock.", "changes every minute — but telling the time is ser"],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[0]}
  caption="Think classify vs. describe, not permanent vs. temporary. Ser says which category something belongs to; estar says what condition or place it is in."
/>

## 5. Adjectives that change meaning

Some adjectives mean one thing with ser and another with estar — the ser meaning is the
characteristic, the estar meaning is the state. These are worth learning as pairs:

<Compare
  columns={['Adjective', 'With ser', 'With estar']}
  rows={[
    ['aburrido', 'Es aburrido. — He is boring.', 'Está aburrido. — He is bored.'],
    ['listo', 'Es lista. — She is clever.', 'Está lista. — She is ready.'],
    ['rico', 'Es rico. — He is rich.', 'Está rico. — It is delicious. (of food)'],
    ['malo', 'Es malo. — He is a bad person.', 'Está malo. — He is ill.'],
    ['verde', 'Es verde. — It is green.', 'Está verde. — It is unripe.'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[1, 2]}
  caption="Same word, two verbs, two meanings. The food one is the one you will hear most — ¡Está muy rico! is the standard compliment to a cook."
/>

<Predict
  question="Someone says 'La sopa está muy rica'. What do they mean?"
  options={['The soup is expensive', 'The soup is delicious', 'The soup is rich in calories', 'The soup is ready']}
  answer={1}
>
  Delicious — rico with estar, of food, means it tastes good. With ser it would mean rich, as in
  wealthy, which soup rarely is.
</Predict>

## 6. The one location that takes ser: events

<Callout kind="warning" title="Things and people are located with estar — events with ser">
La fiesta **es** en mi casa — "the party is at my house". La clase **es** a las diez — "the class
is at ten". An event isn't *in* a place the way a book is on a table; it *takes place* there, and
Spanish treats that as classifying the event. If you can replace "is" with "takes place", it's ser.
</Callout>

<Compare
  columns={['Thing or person → estar', 'Event → ser']}
  rows={[
    ['Ana está en mi casa.', 'La fiesta es en mi casa.'],
    ['El profesor está en la clase.', 'La clase es a las diez.'],
  ]}
  speakColumns={[0, 1]}
/>
