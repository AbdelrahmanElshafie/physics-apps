<Callout kind="why">
Both papers you are working towards are about highly charged ions: **Zr XXXV**, carbon-like
zirconium at $Z = 40$, and **Mo XXXVI**, nitrogen-like molybdenum at $Z = 42$. Every methodological
choice in them — MCDHF rather than Hartree–Fock, Dirac orbitals rather than Schrodinger ones, $jj$
labels rather than $LS$ ones, a Breit term, a QED term — follows from one fact established in this
lesson. Get this fact and the rest of the method stops looking like a pile of acronyms and starts
looking like the only sensible thing to do.
</Callout>

## 1. How fast is a bound electron?

Start with the crudest possible estimate, because it turns out to be right to within a few per
cent. Take a hydrogen-like ion — one electron, nuclear charge $Z$ — and ask how fast that electron
is moving.

<Faded

    },

    },
    {
      why: 'Put molybdenum in the 1s shell: Z = 42, n = 1.',
      latex: String.raw`\frac{v}{c} = \frac{42}{137.036} = 0.3065`,
    },
  ]}
/>

<Eq
  id="v-over-c"
  label="Speed of a bound electron"
  number="11.1"
  latex={String.raw`\frac{v}{c} \approx \frac{Z\alpha}{n}`}
/>

Thirty per cent of the speed of light. That is not a regime where you apply a correction; it
is a regime where you change equations.

<Callout kind="note">
Two things make this estimate trustworthy despite the Bohr model being wrong. First, the virial
theorem gives the same answer for the *root-mean-square* speed in a proper quantum treatment — the
Bohr model gets the scale right even when it gets the picture wrong. Second, nothing here depended
on the details: $v/c = Z\alpha/n$ came out of the Coulomb force and $\hbar$, and those survive into
the real theory unchanged.
</Callout>

## 2. What that speed costs you

Turn the speed into the two numbers that actually enter a calculation.

The **Lorentz factor** tells you how far the electron's mass has departed from $m_e$:

<Eq
  id="lorentz"
  label="Lorentz factor for the 1s electron"
  latex={String.raw`\gamma_{L} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - (Z\alpha)^{2}}}`}
/>

The **fine-structure scale** tells you how large the purely relativistic terms are in energy. For a
hydrogen-like ion the splitting between $2p_{1/2}$ and $2p_{3/2}$ comes out of the Dirac energy
formula as

<Eq
  id="fs-scaling"
  label="Hydrogenic 2p fine structure"
  number="11.2"
  latex={String.raw`\Delta E_{\text{fs}}(2p) = \frac{Z^{4}\alpha^{2}}{16}\,\mathrm{Ry}`}
/>

The $Z^4$ is the whole story of this lesson. Run it across the periodic table:

  columns={['Ion', 'Z', 'v / c', 'Lorentz factor', 'Q / P', '2p fine structure / cm⁻¹']}
  rows={[
    ['H', '1', '0.0073', '1.00003', '0.36%', '0.365'],
    ['C', '6', '0.0438', '1.00096', '2.19%', '473'],
    ['Fe', '26', '0.1897', '1.01850', '9.57%', '$1.67 \\times 10^{5}$'],
    ['Zr — the 2020 paper', '40', '0.2919', '1.04553', '14.92%', '$9.35 \\times 10^{5}$'],
    ['Mo — the 2021 paper', '42', '0.3065', '1.05056', '15.70%', '$1.14 \\times 10^{6}$'],
    ['U', '92', '0.6714', '1.34928', '38.56%', '$2.62 \\times 10^{7}$'],
  ]}
/>

Read the last column again. In hydrogen the $2p$ fine structure is about a third of a wavenumber — a
detail you need a good spectrometer to see. In molybdenum it is over a **million** wavenumbers.
Between those two rows, "fine structure" stopped being fine.

<Callout kind="warning">
A tempting mistake: $(Z\alpha)^2 = 0.094$ at $Z = 42$, which is under ten per cent, so surely
perturbation theory is fine? No — and section 5 is about why. The size of the correction is not
what decides it. What decides it is whether the correction is small *compared to the other terms it
has to compete with*, and in these ions it is not.
</Callout>

## 3. The small component is not small

The Dirac equation replaces the single Schrodinger wavefunction with a four-component spinor. For a
central field it collapses to two radial functions: the **large component** $P(r)$, which goes over
to the Schrodinger radial function as $c \to \infty$, and the **small component** $Q(r)$, which has
no non-relativistic counterpart at all. It vanishes in that limit — hence the name.

For the hydrogen-like ground state both components have exactly the same radial shape,

<Eq
  id="dirac-1s"
  label="Hydrogenic Dirac 1s"
  number="11.3"
  latex={String.raw`P(r) \propto \sqrt{1+\gamma}\; r^{\gamma} e^{-Zr}, \qquad Q(r) \propto -\sqrt{1-\gamma}\; r^{\gamma} e^{-Zr}, \qquad \gamma = \sqrt{1 - (Z\alpha)^{2}}`}
/>

so their ratio is a pure number, independent of $r$:

<Eq
  id="qp-ratio"
  label="Small-to-large ratio"
  number="11.4"
  latex={String.raw`\left\lvert \frac{Q}{P} \right\rvert = \sqrt{\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}}`}
/>

Drag $Z$ and watch it. Two separate things are happening. The orange curve grows — that is
equation 11.4. And the peak of the large component moves in from $1/Z$ to $\gamma/Z$: a
**4.8%** contraction at molybdenum, **25.9%** at uranium. Both are the same $\gamma$.

<DiracRadial
  initialZ={42}
  caption="Hydrogenic Dirac 1s radial functions. The dashed curve is the non-relativistic 1s. The relativistic large component goes as r^gamma with gamma < 1 rather than r^1, which is the contraction; the small component is what a Schrodinger calculation throws away."
/>

Two observations worth carrying forward:

- At $Z = 1$ the small component is 0.36% of the large one and the dashed curve lies almost on top
  of the solid one. Non-relativistic quantum mechanics is not an approximation for hydrogen so much
  as an accurate theory of it.
- At $Z = 42$ the small component is **15.7%**. A method that does not have a $Q(r)$ is not
  approximating this system badly; it does not have the degrees of freedom to describe it.

## 4. Predict before you read on

<Predict
  question="Neutral carbon and Zr XXXV have the same ground configuration, $1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$. Going from carbon ($Z=6$) to zirconium ($Z=40$), roughly what factor does the $2p_{1/2}$–$2p_{3/2}$ splitting grow by?"
  options={[

    'About 2000 — it goes as $Z^4$',
  ]}
  answer={3}
>
Equation 11.2 has $Z^4$ in it, and $(40/6)^4 \approx 1975$. Binding energies go as $Z^2$;
fine structure goes as $Z^4$. That extra factor of $Z^2$ is precisely $(Z\alpha)^2$ — the
relativistic correction — and it is why a term that is invisible in carbon dominates in zirconium.

In the real many-electron ion the screening softens the exponent somewhat, but the conclusion does
not move: the splitting grows by three orders of magnitude while everything else grows by two.
</Predict>

## 5. The coupling scheme itself changes

This is the point where relativity stops being a correction and becomes the organising principle,
and it is the argument the previous callout promised.

A $2p^2$ configuration has two competing energies deciding how the levels arrange themselves:

<Compare
  caption="The competition that sets the coupling scheme"
  columns={['Interaction', 'What it does', 'How it scales with Z']}
  rows={[
    [
      'Residual Coulomb repulsion between the 2p electrons',
      'Splits the configuration into $LS$ terms — $^3P$, $^1D$, $^1S$',
      '$\\propto Z$',
    ],
    [
      'Spin–orbit coupling $\\zeta_{2p}$',
      'Splits each electron into $2p_{1/2}$ and $2p_{3/2}$',
      '$\\propto Z^4$',
    ],
  ]}
/>

Their ratio therefore goes as $Z^3$. At low $Z$ the Coulomb term wins: the electrons first couple
their orbital angular momenta into $L$ and their spins into $S$, and spin–orbit then splits the
resulting term into a small multiplet. That is **$LS$ coupling**, the scheme every undergraduate
course teaches.

Now multiply by $(40/6)^3 \approx 296$. In Zr XXXV the spin–orbit term is hundreds of times more
important relative to the Coulomb term than it is in carbon, and the order reverses: each electron
couples its *own* spin and orbital momentum into $j$ first, and only then do the $j$'s couple to
$J$. That is **$jj$ coupling**.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Why this kills perturbative spin–orbit">
Perturbation theory assumes the perturbation does not reorganise the states it acts on. When
spin–orbit coupling is *larger* than the interaction that defined your basis, the "correction" is
what chooses the zeroth-order states. You cannot compute $LS$ terms and then perturb them, because
in this regime the $LS$ terms were never the right starting point. There is nothing to perturb
*from*.
</Callout>

This is why both papers report levels with $jj$ labels transformed to $LSJ$ ones after the fact,
using `jj2lsj`, and why the $LSJ$ label so often comes with a composition percentage rather than a
clean name. The $LS$ label is a translation, offered for comparison with older literature and with
the NIST tables — not the physics. The physics is $jj$.

That last line is the one with computational teeth. Every relativistic orbital you add is a
separate function to optimise, and the configuration state functions built from them multiply
faster than in the non-relativistic case. The expansions in these papers run to hundreds of
thousands of CSFs, and this is where that number starts.

## 7. So what does the method have to look like?

Everything above forces the shape of the calculation:

| Fact established | What the method must therefore do |
| --- | --- |
| $v/c \approx 0.3$ | Solve the Dirac equation, not Schrodinger's |
| $\lvert Q/P \rvert \approx 16\%$ | Carry both radial components through every step |
| Spin–orbit $\gg$ residual Coulomb | Build the basis in $jj$ coupling from the start |
| $n\ell \to n\ell_j$ | Optimise $2p_{1/2}$ and $2p_{3/2}$ as separate orbitals |
| Levels are strongly mixed | Use a multiconfiguration expansion, not a single determinant |

Those five lines are, in order, the Dirac equation, the large/small component pair, $jj$ coupling,
the relativistic orbital set, and multiconfiguration — which is to say, **MCDHF**. The acronym is
not a choice of technique among several. It is the list of things this ion leaves you no option
about.
