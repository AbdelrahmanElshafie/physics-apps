<Callout kind="why">
Last lesson ended on an accident: written in one basis the spin-flip matrix was diagonal, and
there the operator did nothing but multiply each component by a number. This lesson is about
finding that basis deliberately, for any operator. It is the most practically useful thing in
Module 1.1 — and in quantum mechanics those numbers are not a convenience, they are the possible
results of a measurement.
</Callout>

## 1. The question

Most directions get rotated *and* stretched by an operator. A few get only stretched.

<Eq
  id="eigen-equation"
  label="The eigenvalue equation"
  latex={String.raw`\hat{A}\ket{\psi} = \lambda \ket{\psi}`}
/>

A ket satisfying this is an **eigenvector** of $\hat{A}$, and the number $\lambda$ is its
**eigenvalue**. The equation says: acting with the operator did nothing that scaling would not
have done.

Drag the blue arrow below. The gold arrow is where the operator sends it. Most of the time they
disagree; on the dashed lines they do not.

<EigenPlot
  matrix={[
    [0, 1],
    [1, 0],
  ]}
  initial={[3, 1]}
  label="σₓ"
  caption="The spin-flip operator. Two dashed eigendirections, with eigenvalues +1 and -1."
/>

<Callout kind="note">
If $\ket{\psi}$ is an eigenvector then so is $c\ket{\psi}$ for any non-zero $c$ — the same equation
survives multiplication. So an eigenvector is really a **direction**, not a particular arrow, and
we fix the length afterwards by normalising.
</Callout>

## 2. Finding them

Rearrange the eigenvalue equation so one side is zero:

<Eq
  id="rearranged"
  latex={String.raw`\left(\hat{A} - \lambda\mathbb{1}\right)\ket{\psi} = 0`}
/>

This says the operator $\hat{A} - \lambda\mathbb{1}$ annihilates a non-zero vector. An operator
that does that cannot be invertible — and a matrix is non-invertible exactly when its determinant
vanishes:

<Eq
  id="characteristic"
  label="Characteristic equation"
  latex={String.raw`\det\left(A - \lambda \mathbb{1}\right) = 0`}
/>

For an $N \times N$ matrix this is a polynomial of degree $N$ in $\lambda$, so there are $N$
eigenvalues counted with multiplicity — possibly complex, possibly repeated.

<Derivation caption="Eigenvalues of the spin-flip operator">
  <Step
    why="Subtract lambda from the diagonal."
    latex={String.raw`A - \lambda\mathbb{1} = \begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Take the determinant: product of the diagonal minus product of the off-diagonal."
    latex={String.raw`\det = \lambda^2 - 1`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Set it to zero and solve."
    latex={String.raw`\lambda^2 - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda = \pm 1`}
  />
</Derivation>

Now put each eigenvalue back to find its direction.

<Derivation caption="The eigenvector for lambda = +1">
  <Step
    why="Substitute lambda = 1 into (A - lambda)psi = 0."
    latex={String.raw`\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = 0`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Both rows give the same condition — they must, or only the zero vector would satisfy it."
    latex={String.raw`-v_1 + v_2 = 0 \implies v_2 = v_1`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Any vector with equal components works. Normalise to fix the length."
    latex={String.raw`\ket{+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}`}
  />
</Derivation>

Repeating for $\lambda = -1$ gives $v_2 = -v_1$, that is $\ket{-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1)$.
Those are exactly the basis vectors that made the matrix diagonal last lesson — and now they were
*derived* rather than guessed.

<Callout kind="warning">
The two rows agreeing is a feature, not a mistake in your algebra. The determinant was set to zero
precisely to make the rows dependent. If you ever get two independent conditions, the eigenvalue
is wrong.
</Callout>

## 3. Degeneracy

When an eigenvalue repeats it is **degenerate**, and it may come with a whole plane of
eigenvectors rather than a single direction.

The identity operator is the extreme case: every vector satisfies
$\mathbb{1}\ket{\psi} = 1\ket{\psi}$, so $\lambda = 1$ with the entire space as its eigenspace. Any
basis at all diagonalises it.

<Callout kind="forward">
Degeneracy is not a curiosity in nuclear physics — it is the norm. A level with angular momentum
$j$ is $(2j+1)$-fold degenerate, and lifting that degeneracy with a magnetic field or a deformation
is how much of nuclear structure gets probed. When it happens you are free to choose any
orthonormal basis within the eigenspace, and the art is choosing one that respects another
symmetry.
</Callout>

## 4. Diagonalisation

Collect the eigenvectors as the columns of $U$ and the eigenvalues down the diagonal of $\Lambda$:

<Eq
  id="diagonalisation"
  label="Diagonalisation"
  latex={String.raw`A = U \Lambda U^{\dagger} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \Lambda = U^{\dagger} A U`}
/>

This is the change of basis from last lesson, with the basis chosen to be the eigenvectors. In that
basis the operator is as simple as an operator can be.

That third row is the reason this topic is on your checklist. A set of coupled channel equations
becomes, in the eigenbasis, a set of independent ones — which is exactly how a coupled-channels
calculation is made tractable.

## 5. A free consistency check

The invariants from last lesson can be read straight off the eigenvalues:

<Eq
  id="trace-det"
  label="Invariants from eigenvalues"
  latex={String.raw`\operatorname{tr} A = \sum_i \lambda_i \qquad \det A = \prod_i \lambda_i`}
/>

For the spin-flip operator: trace $0 = (+1) + (-1)$, determinant $-1 = (+1)(-1)$. Both check out.

<Callout kind="note">
Use this. It costs nothing and catches most arithmetic slips: add your eigenvalues and compare
with the trace, multiply them and compare with the determinant. If either disagrees, you have made
a mistake before you have wasted time on the eigenvectors.
</Callout>

## 6. What the numbers mean

In quantum mechanics the eigenvalue equation is not a computational trick — it is the measurement
postulate.

    ['$\\hat{H}\\ket{E} = E\\ket{E}$', 'the stationary states and their energies'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="forward">
One thing should now look fragile. Nothing so far guarantees the eigenvalues are **real** — the
characteristic polynomial can perfectly well have complex roots — and a measurement had better not
return $3 + 2i$. Nor is there any guarantee the eigenvectors form a basis at all.

**Next: Hermitian operators**, which are exactly the class for which both guarantees hold. That is
why every observable is one, and it is the last structural piece before commutators.
</Callout>
