<Callout kind="why">
You have already met an operator: the projector $\ket{e}\bra{e}$. This lesson asks what operators
are in general. The payoff is one unfamiliar fact — **applying two operators in the other order
can give a different answer** — and that single fact is where the uncertainty principle,
quantised angular momentum and the whole structure of the next five topics come from.
</Callout>

## 1. What an operator is

<Definition term="Operator">
A rule that takes a ket and returns a ket: $\hat{A}\ket{\psi} = \ket{\varphi}$. The hat is a
reminder that it is an instruction, not a number.
</Definition>

An operator is **linear** when it respects superposition:

<Eq
  id="linearity"
  label="The linearity condition"
  latex={String.raw`\hat{A}\left(\alpha\ket{\psi} + \beta\ket{\phi}\right) = \alpha\hat{A}\ket{\psi} + \beta\hat{A}\ket{\phi}`}
/>

That is the entire definition, and it is doing more work than it looks. Quantum states superpose;
an operator that failed this would take a legitimate superposition to something that depended on
how you chose to decompose it. Linearity is what keeps the physics consistent.

Notice that $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$ are utterly different kinds of instruction — one multiplies,
one differentiates — yet both are linear, so both are legitimate operators.

## 3. Testing linearity

Check the two halves separately: does it survive addition, and does it survive scaling?

<Compare
  caption="Linear or not?"
  columns={['Rule', 'Linear?', 'Why']}
  rows={[
    ['$f \\mapsto x f$', 'yes', 'multiplication distributes over sums'],
    ['$f \\mapsto \\dd f/\\dd x$', 'yes', 'differentiation is linear'],
    ['$f \\mapsto f(-x)$', 'yes', 'relabelling the argument does not mix terms'],
    ['$f \\mapsto f^2$', 'no', '$(f+g)^2 \\neq f^2 + g^2$'],
    ['$f \\mapsto f + 1$', 'no', 'fails scaling: doubling the input does not double the output'],
    ['$f \\mapsto f^{*}$', 'no', 'pulls a scalar out conjugated — *antilinear*'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="note">
Complex conjugation is the interesting failure. It survives addition but conjugates any scalar it
pulls out, which makes it **antilinear** rather than merely broken. Time reversal is antilinear
for exactly this reason — it is the one place in physics where this distinction matters.
</Callout>

## 4. Operator algebra

Operators add, scale, and multiply — where multiplication means *do one, then the other*.

<Axioms
  caption="How operators combine"
  items={[
    ['(\\hat{A} + \\hat{B})\\ket{\\psi} = \\hat{A}\\ket{\\psi} + \\hat{B}\\ket{\\psi}', 'sum: apply both, add results'],
    ['(c\\hat{A})\\ket{\\psi} = c\\left(\\hat{A}\\ket{\\psi}\\right)', 'scalar multiple'],
    ['\\hat{A}\\hat{B}\\ket{\\psi} = \\hat{A}\\left(\\hat{B}\\ket{\\psi}\\right)', 'product: B first, then A'],
    ['\\hat{A}^2 = \\hat{A}\\hat{A}', 'powers are repeated application'],
    ['\\hat{A}^{-1}\\hat{A} = \\mathbb{1}', 'the inverse undoes it, when one exists'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
Read $\hat{A}\hat{B}$ **right to left**: $\hat{B}$ acts first. It looks backwards, and it is the
commonest slip when first computing a commutator.
</Callout>

## 5. Order matters

For numbers, $ab = ba$ always. For operators it is the exception, not the rule.

<Derivation caption="Applying x and d/dx in both orders">
  <Step
    why="First order: differentiate, then multiply by x."
    latex={String.raw`\hat{x}\frac{\dd}{\dd x} f = x f'`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Other order: multiply by x first, then differentiate — and the product rule bites."
    latex={String.raw`\frac{\dd}{\dd x} \hat{x} f = \frac{\dd}{\dd x}\left(x f\right) = f + x f'`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Subtract. The x f' terms cancel and a whole extra function is left over."
    latex={String.raw`\left(\hat{x}\frac{\dd}{\dd x} - \frac{\dd}{\dd x}\hat{x}\right) f = x f' - f - x f' = -f`}
  />
  <Step
    why="True for every f, so this is a statement about the operators themselves."
    latex={String.raw`\left[\hat{x}, \frac{\dd}{\dd x}\right] = -1`}
  />
</Derivation>

The bracket is the **commutator**, $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = \hat{A}\hat{B} - \hat{B}\hat{A}$. It
measures the failure of two operators to commute — zero when order does not matter.

Put in the physical momentum operator $\hat{p} = -i\hbar\,\dd/\dd x$ and the same calculation gives
the most important equation in quantum mechanics:

<Eq
  id="canonical-commutator"
  label="The canonical commutator"
  latex={String.raw`\left[\hat{x}, \hat{p}\right] = i\hbar`}
/>

<Callout kind="forward">
That $\hbar$ on the right is not decoration. Non-zero commutator means position and momentum
cannot both be sharp, and the uncertainty principle is a theorem about exactly this number. The
Commutators topic later in this module derives the consequences; for now the point is only that
operators can fail to commute, and that the failure is itself a physical quantity.
</Callout>

## 6. The adjoint

Every operator has a partner, defined by how it can be moved across a bracket.

<Eq
  id="adjoint-definition"
  label="Defining the adjoint"
  latex={String.raw`\braoket{\phi}{\hat{A}}{\psi} = \braoket{\psi}{\hat{A}^{\dagger}}{\phi}^{*}`}
/>

Read it as: acting with $\hat{A}$ to the right is the same as acting with $\hat{A}^{\dagger}$ to
the left, once you conjugate. In components it is the plainest thing imaginable — transpose and
conjugate:

<Eq
  id="adjoint-matrix"
  label="The adjoint in components"
  latex={String.raw`\left(A^{\dagger}\right)_{mn} = \left(A_{nm}\right)^{*}`}
/>

<Axioms
  caption="Rules for the dagger"
  items={[
    ['\\left(\\hat{A}^{\\dagger}\\right)^{\\dagger} = \\hat{A}', 'applying it twice returns the original'],
    ['\\left(\\hat{A} + \\hat{B}\\right)^{\\dagger} = \\hat{A}^{\\dagger} + \\hat{B}^{\\dagger}', 'it distributes over sums'],
    ['\\left(c\\hat{A}\\right)^{\\dagger} = c^{*}\\hat{A}^{\\dagger}', 'scalars come out conjugated'],
    ['\\left(\\hat{A}\\hat{B}\\right)^{\\dagger} = \\hat{B}^{\\dagger}\\hat{A}^{\\dagger}', 'products reverse order'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
The last rule catches people out. $(\hat{A}\hat{B})^{\dagger}$ is $\hat{B}^{\dagger}\hat{A}^{\dagger}$,
**not** $\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{B}^{\dagger}$. The same reversal you already know from transposing
a matrix product.
</Callout>

## 7. The families worth naming

Three special cases carry almost all the physics, and each gets its own topic later.

<Compare
  caption="Operator families"
  columns={['Family', 'Condition', 'What it means physically']}
  rows={[
    ['Hermitian', '$\\hat{A}^{\\dagger} = \\hat{A}$', 'observables — real measured values'],
    ['Unitary', '$\\hat{U}^{\\dagger}\\hat{U} = \\mathbb{1}$', 'symmetries and time evolution — probability preserved'],
    ['Projector', '$\\hat{P}^2 = \\hat{P} = \\hat{P}^{\\dagger}$', 'measurement outcomes, basis components'],
  ]}
/>

One line of argument shows why observables must be Hermitian. Set $\phi = \psi$ in the definition
of the adjoint:

<Derivation caption="Why Hermitian means real">
  <Step
    why="Take the expectation value and apply the adjoint definition with phi = psi."
    latex={String.raw`\braoket{\psi}{\hat{A}}{\psi} = \braoket{\psi}{\hat{A}^{\dagger}}{\psi}^{*}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="If A is Hermitian the two operators are the same, so the number equals its own conjugate."
    latex={String.raw`\hat{A}^{\dagger} = \hat{A} \implies \langle \hat{A} \rangle = \langle \hat{A} \rangle^{*}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Only real numbers equal their own conjugate. A measured average had better be real."
    latex={String.raw`\implies \langle \hat{A} \rangle \in \mathbb{R}`}
  />
</Derivation>

Unitarity has an equally short story: if $\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{U} = \mathbb{1}$ then
$\braket{\hat{U}\phi}{\hat{U}\psi} = \braket{\phi}{\psi}$, so every overlap survives the
transformation unchanged. Total probability is one overlap among many, so it survives too — which
is why time evolution and every change of basis is unitary.

<Callout kind="forward">
Next: **matrix representation**. Choose a basis, compute $A_{mn} = \braoket{e_m}{\hat{A}}{e_n}$,
and every abstract statement above becomes an array of numbers you can hand to a computer. That
is the step that turns the optical model from a page of operators into a calculation.
</Callout>
