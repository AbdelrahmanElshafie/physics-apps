<Callout kind="why">
You already have the definition of an inner product. This lesson is about what it *does* once you
stop thinking of vectors as short columns of numbers. Three moves: a function is a vector, a basis
can be continuous, and if nobody hands you an orthonormal basis you can build one. The third move
turns out to produce the Legendre polynomials, which run through the rest of this syllabus.
</Callout>

## 1. A function is a vector

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is three numbers, indexed by $1, 2, 3$. A function $f(x)$ is a number
for every $x$ — indexed by a continuous label instead of a discrete one.

<Compare
  caption="The same object, two kinds of index"
  columns={['', 'Column vector', 'Function']}
  rows={[
    ['Index', '$n = 1, 2, \\ldots, N$', '$x$, continuous'],
    ['Entries', '$v_n$', '$f(x)$'],
    ['Add them', '$u_n + v_n$', '$f(x) + g(x)$'],
    ['Scale', '$c\\,v_n$', '$c\\,f(x)$'],
    ['Inner product', '$\\sum_n u_n^{*} v_n$', '$\\int f^{*}(x)\\,g(x)\\,\\dd x$'],
  ]}
/>

Every row matches. The sum becomes an integral because the index became continuous — that is the
*whole* difference.

<Eq
  id="function-inner-product"
  label="Inner product of two functions"
  latex={String.raw`\braket{f}{g} = \int_a^b f^{*}(x)\, g(x)\, \dd x`}
/>

<Callout kind="note">
The interval $[a,b]$ is part of the definition, not an afterthought. Two functions can be
orthogonal on $[-1,1]$ and not orthogonal on $[0,1]$. Change the interval and you have changed
the space.
</Callout>

## 2. Orthogonality you can compute

<Derivation caption="Are sin x and cos x orthogonal on [-pi, pi]?">
  <Step
    why="Write down the inner product."
    latex={String.raw`\braket{\sin}{\cos} = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin x \cos x \, \dd x`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Use the double-angle identity to turn a product into a single sine."
    latex={String.raw`= \tfrac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin 2x \, \dd x`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Sine is odd, and the interval is symmetric about the origin, so the halves cancel."
    latex={String.raw`= 0 \implies \sin \perp \cos`}
  />
</Derivation>

That orthogonality is exactly why a Fourier series works: each coefficient can be extracted with a
single integral, with no interference from the other terms. It is the same
$c_n = \braket{e_n}{\psi}$ move from two lessons ago, in a function space.

## 3. Weight functions

Sometimes the natural inner product carries an extra factor:

<Eq
  id="weighted-inner-product"
  label="Weighted inner product"
  latex={String.raw`\braket{f}{g}_w = \int_a^b f^{*}(x)\, g(x)\, w(x)\, \dd x`}
/>

The weight $w(x)$ must be positive, so that $\braket{f}{f}_w > 0$ still holds and the axioms
survive. This looks like a technicality and is not: the classical polynomial families are each
orthogonal with respect to a *different* weight.

<Compare
  caption="Families you will meet in Module 1.2"
  columns={['Family', 'Interval', 'Weight $w(x)$', 'Where it appears']}
  rows={[
    ['Legendre $P_l$', '$[-1, 1]$', '$1$', 'angular momentum, partial waves'],
    ['Chebyshev $T_n$', '$[-1, 1]$', '$1/\\sqrt{1-x^2}$', 'numerical approximation'],
    ['Laguerre $L_n$', '$[0, \\infty)$', '$e^{-x}$', 'radial hydrogen-like problems'],
    ['Hermite $H_n$', '$(-\\infty, \\infty)$', '$e^{-x^2}$', 'the harmonic oscillator'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="forward">
Sturm-Liouville theory, in Module 1.2, explains why each family comes with its own weight: the
weight is whatever makes the relevant differential operator self-adjoint, and self-adjoint
operators have orthogonal eigenfunctions. The orthogonality is not a coincidence to memorise —
it is forced.
</Callout>

## 4. Continuous bases and the delta

A discrete basis satisfies $\braket{e_m}{e_n} = \delta_{mn}$. What replaces that when the label is
continuous, as with position?

<Eq
  id="delta-normalisation"
  label="Continuous orthonormality"
  latex={String.raw`\braket{x}{x'} = \delta(x - x')`}
/>

<Eq
  id="continuous-completeness"
  label="Completeness, continuous form"
  latex={String.raw`\int \ket{x}\bra{x} \, \dd x = \mathbb{1}`}
/>

The Kronecker delta became a Dirac delta, and the sum became an integral. Everything else is the
same — including the trick of inserting the identity.

<Derivation caption="Recovering the integral formula from the Dirac machinery">
  <Step why="Start from the abstract overlap of two states." latex={String.raw`\braket{\phi}{\psi}`} />
  <Step
    why="Insert the continuous identity, exactly as with a discrete basis."
    latex={String.raw`= \bra{\phi} \left( \int \ket{x}\bra{x} \, \dd x \right) \ket{\psi}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Regroup into brackets."
    latex={String.raw`= \int \braket{\phi}{x} \braket{x}{\psi} \, \dd x`}
  />
  <Step
    why="And each bracket is a wavefunction value, with the bra supplying the conjugate."
    latex={String.raw`= \int \phi^{*}(x)\, \psi(x) \, \dd x`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Callout kind="note">
That derivation is the point of Dirac notation in one figure. The integral formula for the inner
product of two wavefunctions is not a separate definition to learn — it is the abstract bracket
with the position identity inserted.
</Callout>

## 5. Gram-Schmidt: building an orthonormal basis

Often you are handed a basis that spans the space but is neither orthogonal nor normalised.
Gram-Schmidt fixes that, one vector at a time: take the next vector, **subtract off everything
that already points along the directions you have**, and normalise what is left.

<Eq
  id="gram-schmidt"
  label="Gram-Schmidt step"
  latex={String.raw`u_k = v_k - \sum_{j<k} \braket{e_j}{v_k}\, e_j \qquad\text{then}\qquad e_k = \frac{u_k}{\lVert u_k \rVert}`}
/>

The subtracted term is a sum of projections — the same projection operator from the last lesson,
applied once per direction already fixed.

### Worked: where Legendre polynomials come from

Apply it to the least imaginative basis imaginable, $\{1,\, x,\, x^2\}$ on $[-1,1]$ with weight 1.

<Derivation caption="Gram-Schmidt on 1, x, x squared">
  <Step
    why="First vector: just normalise it. The inner product of 1 with itself is the interval length, 2."
    latex={String.raw`\braket{1}{1} = \int_{-1}^{1} \dd x = 2 \implies e_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Second: check the overlap with e1 first. The integrand is odd, so it vanishes."
    latex={String.raw`\braket{e_1}{x} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{-1}^{1} x \, \dd x = 0`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Nothing to subtract, so x is already orthogonal to the constant. Normalise it."
    latex={String.raw`\braket{x}{x} = \int_{-1}^{1} x^2 \dd x = \tfrac{2}{3} \implies e_2 = x\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Third: x squared does overlap the constant, so this time there is something to remove."
    latex={String.raw`\braket{e_1}{x^2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{-1}^{1} x^2 \dd x = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{3}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Its overlap with e2 vanishes, since x cubed is odd."
    latex={String.raw`\braket{e_2}{x^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1} x^3 \dd x = 0`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Subtract the one non-zero projection."
    latex={String.raw`u_3 = x^2 - \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = x^2 - \tfrac{1}{3}`}
  />
</Derivation>

Now compare that last line with the second Legendre polynomial:

<Eq
  id="legendre-two"
  label="The second Legendre polynomial"
  latex={String.raw`P_2(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(3x^2 - 1\right) = \tfrac{3}{2}\left(x^2 - \tfrac{1}{3}\right)`}
/>

Identical up to a constant factor.

<Callout kind="why">
Nothing about Legendre polynomials was assumed. Orthogonalising $\{1, x, x^2, \ldots\}$ on
$[-1,1]$ *produces* them. They are not a special function someone invented — they are what the
powers of $x$ become once you insist they be orthogonal on that interval. Every Legendre identity
in Module 1.2 is a fact about that construction.
</Callout>

## 6. Truncation is the best approximation

Keep only the first $N$ terms of an expansion and you get the closest vector in that subspace —
closest in the distance the inner product defines.

<Eq
  id="best-approximation"
  label="Best approximation"
  latex={String.raw`\psi_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \braket{e_n}{\psi}\, \ket{e_n} \quad\text{minimises}\quad \lVert \psi - \psi_N \rVert`}
/>

The error is whatever is left over, and it is orthogonal to everything you kept. This is why
truncating a partial-wave sum at some maximum $l$ is a controlled approximation rather than a
guess: the discarded terms are orthogonal to the retained ones, so they cannot quietly corrupt
them.
