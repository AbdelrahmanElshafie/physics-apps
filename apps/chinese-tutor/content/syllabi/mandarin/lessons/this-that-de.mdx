Two small words for pointing at things, and one particle that glues a description onto a noun.
Between them they cover most of what English does with "this," "that," "my," "'s" and "of" — and
the pointing words obey exactly the rule the last lesson introduced for numbers.

## 1. 这 and 那

<VocabCard hanzi="这" pinyin="zhè" meaning="this" />
<VocabCard hanzi="那" pinyin="nà" meaning="that" />

On their own, as the subject of a sentence, they behave like English:

<Dialogue
  caption="Pointing at things"
  lines={[
    { speaker: 'A', hanzi: '这是什么?', pinyin: 'Zhè shì shénme?', meaning: 'What is this?' },
    { speaker: 'B', hanzi: '那是我的书。', pinyin: 'Nà shì wǒ de shū.', meaning: 'That is my book.' },
  ]}
/>

## 2. In front of a noun, they need a measure word

<GrammarPoint pattern="这 / 那 + measure word + noun" title="Same rule as numbers">

这**本**书 — "this book." 那**个**人 — "that person." 这书 on its own is wrong for exactly the
reason 三书 was: a measure word has to stand between the pointing word and the noun. The slot
that a number fills in 三本书 is the same slot 这 fills in 这本书.

</GrammarPoint>

<Callout kind="why" title="Why this is the same rule and not a second one">
It helps to see 这, 那 and the numbers as one family: they all *specify* a noun — how many, or
which one — and in Mandarin anything that specifies a noun that way goes through a measure word.
That's why the two can even stack: 这三本书, "these three books," with 这 and 三 both in front of
the one measure word.
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="How would you say 'that cat', given the measure word for animals is 只?"
  options={['那猫', '那只猫', '只那猫', '那个只猫']}
  answer={1}
>
  那只猫 — pointing word, measure word, noun. 那猫 skips the measure word, which the previous
  lesson established is not an option.
</Predict>

### 哪 — which

<VocabCard hanzi="哪" pinyin="nǎ" meaning="which" note="Looks like 那 with a 口 added, and sounds like it with a different tone — nǎ (third) rather than nà (fourth). It follows the same measure-word rule: 哪本书? — which book?" />

## 3. 的 — possession, and more

<VocabCard hanzi="的" pinyin="de" meaning="the possessive / linking particle" note="Neutral tone, and one of the most frequent characters in written Chinese." />

<GrammarPoint pattern="owner + 的 + thing" title="Apostrophe-s and 'of', in one word">

我的书 — "my book." 他的猫 — "his cat." 老师的名字 — "the teacher's name," which is also "the
name of the teacher": English has two constructions for this and Mandarin has one. The word before
的 owns or describes; the word after it is the thing.

</GrammarPoint>

<Compare
  columns={['Mandarin', 'English']}
  rows={[
    ['我的书', 'my book'],
    ['他的猫', 'his cat'],
    ['老师的名字', "the teacher's name / the name of the teacher"],
    ['我的', 'mine (the noun left off — see below)'],
  ]}
/>

### Two things worth knowing about 的

**It can be dropped for close relationships.** 我妈妈 ("my mum") and 我朋友 ("my friend") are at
least as common as 我的妈妈 and 我的朋友 — the 的 is optional when the relationship is personal and
obvious. It's never *wrong* to include it; it's just often left out.

<VocabCard hanzi="朋友" pinyin="péngyǒu" meaning="friend" />

**The noun after it can be omitted when it's already clear.** 这是我的 — "this is mine." The 书 is
understood from context, and 的 is left standing at the end, carrying "mine" by itself.

<Predict
  question="Someone holds up a book and you want to say 'That's mine.' Which is it?"
  options={['那是我', '那是我的', '那的是我', '那是的我']}
  answer={1}
>
  那是我的. Without 的, 那是我 would mean "that is me." The 的 is what turns 我 into "mine," and the
  noun can be left off because everyone can see the book.
</Predict>
