Two topics in one lesson, because the second needs the first to have anything to count. Pronouns
are mercifully simple; measure words are the part with no English equivalent — except, as it turns
out, the part of English you already use without noticing.

## 1. Pronouns

<Compare

  rows={[
    ['我', 'wǒ', 'I / me', '我们 wǒmen — we / us'],
    ['你', 'nǐ', 'you', '你们 nǐmen — you (plural)'],
    ['他', 'tā', 'he / him', '他们 tāmen — they / them'],

  caption="Plural is formed by adding 们 (men) — one rule, no exceptions."
/>

<Callout kind="why" title="No I/me distinction at all">
English changes the word depending on the job: I/me, he/him, they/them. Mandarin doesn't. 我 is
我 whether it's doing the action or receiving it — 我看他 ("I see him") and 他看我 ("he sees me")
use the same two pronouns, with only the order telling you who did what. One less thing to learn,
and a hint about how much work word order does in Mandarin.
</Callout>

### 他, 她 and 它 are the same word out loud

All three are pronounced **tā**. The distinction exists only on paper — and it's built exactly the
way the radicals lesson described:

<Compare
  columns={['Character', 'Radical', 'Refers to']}
  rows={[
    ['他', '亻 person', 'he / him'],
    ['她', '女 woman', 'she / her'],
    ['它', '宀 roof', 'it — animals and things'],
  ]}
  caption="他 and 她 share the component 也, which supplies the sound; the radical on the left supplies the meaning. Spoken aloud, context alone tells you which is meant."
/>

>
  No. 他 and 她 are both tā, with the same tone and the same vowel. The writing system distinguishes
  them; the spoken language doesn't, and speakers rely entirely on context — which is rarely a
  problem in practice.
</Predict>

## 2. Measure words: the part with no direct English equivalent

Here is the rule: **you cannot put a number directly in front of a noun.** 三书 ("three book") is
not a sentence with a small error in it — it's not Mandarin. Something has to go in between:

<GrammarPoint pattern="number + measure word + noun" title="The counting pattern">

三**本**书 — "three books," where 本 (běn) is the measure word that goes with bound volumes.
一**个**人 — "one person," with the general-purpose 个. The middle word is not optional.

</GrammarPoint>

<Callout kind="why" title="English does this too — just not for everything">
You already say "three **sheets** of paper," "two **cups** of tea," "a **loaf** of bread," "five
**head** of cattle." English requires a measure word for things it treats as uncountable, and
skips it for things it treats as countable. Mandarin simply applies the same mechanism to *every*
noun. It isn't a strange new concept — it's a familiar one with the exceptions removed.
</Callout>

## 4. Where 两 finally makes sense

The last lesson introduced 两 (liǎng) as a second word for "two" without fully explaining when it
applies. This is when:

<GrammarPoint pattern="两个人 ✓   ·   二个人 ✗" title="两 is the counting-things two">

The moment a number sits in front of a measure word, "two" is 两, not 二. 二 is for reciting
numbers and for building larger ones (二十二); 两 is for two actual things.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="How would you say 'two cats', given that the measure word for animals is 只?"
  options={['二只猫', '两只猫', '两猫', '只两猫']}
  answer={1}
>
  两只猫 — 两 because we're counting real things, 只 because they're animals, and the noun last.
  Both 二只猫 (wrong "two") and 两猫 (no measure word) would be wrong.
</Predict>
