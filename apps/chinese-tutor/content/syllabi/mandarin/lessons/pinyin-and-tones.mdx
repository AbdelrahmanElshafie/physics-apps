Chinese characters don't spell out their own pronunciation — a character like 好 gives no clue,
just by looking at it, that it's said "hǎo". **Pinyin** is the standard system for writing that
pronunciation using the Latin alphabet, so every lesson in this course gives a word three ways:
the character, its pinyin, and its meaning.

<VocabCard hanzi="你好" pinyin="nǐ hǎo" meaning="hello" />

## 1. Pinyin is spelling, not meaning

Pinyin syllables are made of an **initial** (the consonant sound a syllable starts with, if any)
and a **final** (everything after it — a vowel or vowel-plus-consonant). "Hǎo" breaks down as
initial `h` + final `ǎo`.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Pinyin letters don't always sound like English">
Some pinyin letters are pronounced nothing like their English equivalent. `q` sounds roughly like
the "ch" in "cheese", `x` is a soft "sh", and `c` is like "ts" in "cats". Learning pinyin is
learning a new code, not reading English with an accent.
</Callout>

## 2. Tone is part of the word

This is the part that trips up almost every new learner: in Mandarin, the pitch contour you say a
syllable with is not optional expression — it's part of the word's identity, exactly the way a
vowel is. Change the tone and you get a different word, not the same word said differently.

The syllable "ma" means four completely different things depending on tone:

## 3. The four tones

<GrammarPoint pattern="mā · má · mǎ · mà" title="The four tones, as pitch shapes">

1. **First tone (ā)** — flat and high, held steady, like holding a single musical note.
2. **Second tone (á)** — rising, like the pitch at the end of an English question: "Really?"
3. **Third tone (ǎ)** — falls then rises, dipping low before coming back up. In isolation it's the
   longest tone; in connected speech it's often shortened to just the low dip (more on that in the
   next lesson, on tone pairs).
4. **Fourth tone (à)** — falling sharply, like a short, firm command: "Stop!"

There's also a **neutral tone** (no mark at all, as in the question particle 吗 "ma"), said lightly
and quickly, borrowing its pitch from whatever came before it.

</GrammarPoint>

## 4. Reading the marks

The tone mark sits on the main vowel of the syllable. Numbered pinyin (`ma1`, `ma2`, `ma3`, `ma4`,
`ma5`) is the keyboard-friendly stand-in you'll type when there's no accent key handy — this course
uses it for every typed answer, and the toned form (mā, má, mǎ, mà, ma) for everything you read.
