---
title: Mandarin word order
summary: Subject-Verb-Object, mostly — and the handful of places it isn't.
readingMinutes: 25
---

Good news first: the basic sentence shape is the same as English. Subject, then verb, then object.
The lesson is really about the two places where Mandarin puts things somewhere English doesn't —
and both of them move words *earlier* in the sentence than an English speaker expects.

## 1. The baseline: subject – verb – object

<VocabCard hanzi="看" pinyin="kàn" meaning="to look at, read, watch" />
<VocabCard hanzi="饭" pinyin="fàn" meaning="food; a meal" note="吃饭 (chī fàn), 'eat a meal', is the everyday way to say 'eat' when there's no particular food in mind." />

<GrammarPoint pattern="他看书。" title="Same order as English">

他看书 — "He reads / is reading a book." 他 subject, 看 verb, 书 object. 我吃饭, 我要茶, 我有书 —
every sentence so far has quietly followed this order, and most sentences you'll ever build will
too.

</GrammarPoint>

## 2. Time goes before the verb

<VocabCard hanzi="今天" pinyin="jīntiān" meaning="today" />

English lets a time word sit at the end: "I'm eating *today*." Mandarin does not. A time word goes
**before the verb** — either right after the subject or at the very start of the sentence:

<Compare
  columns={['Mandarin', 'Status']}
  rows={[
    ['我今天吃饭。', '✓ subject, time, verb'],
    ['今天我吃饭。', '✓ time first, then subject — slightly more emphasis on "today"'],
    ['我吃饭今天。', '✗ time after the object — not Mandarin'],
  ]}
  caption="Both correct versions put 今天 before 吃. The only rule is that it never comes after the verb."
/>

## 3. Place goes before the verb too

<VocabCard hanzi="在" pinyin="zài" meaning="at / in — introduces a place" />
<VocabCard hanzi="家" pinyin="jiā" meaning="home" />

Same pattern: "I eat *at home*" ends with the place in English, but Mandarin says where before it
says what:

<GrammarPoint pattern="我在家吃饭。" title="在 + place, then the verb">

我在家吃饭 — literally "I at-home eat." 在家 comes before 吃饭, not after it. 我吃饭在家 is the
mistake every English speaker makes first, because it's a direct translation.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="Which is correct for 'I eat at home today'?"
  options={['我吃饭在家今天', '我今天在家吃饭', '我在家吃饭今天', '今天在家我吃饭']}
  answer={1}
>
  我今天在家吃饭 — subject, time, place, verb. Both 今天 and 在家 stay in front of 吃饭. The first
  and third options are English order with Mandarin words; the last has the subject in the wrong
  place.
</Predict>

<Callout kind="why" title="When and where before what">
If you remember one thing from this lesson: in Mandarin, you set the scene before the action.
Time and place are *scene*, so they come first; the verb and its object are the action, so they
come last. English does it the other way round for time and place, which is exactly why those two
are where translation goes wrong — everything else in the sentence lines up.
</Callout>

## 5. What doesn't change the order

Questions don't invert anything. 你是学生 ("you are a student") becomes 你是学生吗 ("are you a
student?") by adding 吗 at the end — the next lesson is about this — and question words like 什么
sit exactly where the answer would go. Nothing moves. Negation doesn't move anything either: 不
and 没 go directly in front of the verb, and the rest stays put.
