---
title: "Negation with 不 and 没"
summary: Two different "not"s, and the rule for which one a sentence needs.
readingMinutes: 15
---

English has one "not" and attaches it to whatever auxiliary happens to be around: *don't*,
*didn't*, *isn't*, *haven't*. Mandarin has two, and which one you use isn't about the auxiliary —
there are none — but about *what kind of thing* is being denied.

## 1. 不 — the default "not"

<VocabCard hanzi="不" pinyin="bù" meaning="not — for states, habits, intentions and adjectives" note="bú before a fourth-tone syllable, as the tone-pairs lesson established: 不是 bú shì, 不要 bú yào." />

不 goes directly in front of the verb or adjective, and covers everything that is a matter of
*what is the case* — now, habitually, or in the future:

<Compare
  columns={['Mandarin', 'Means', 'Kind of thing denied']}
  rows={[
    ['我不是学生。', "I'm not a student.", 'a state (是)'],
    ['他不忙。', "He isn't busy.", 'an adjective — and no 很'],
    ['我不要。', "I don't want it.", 'an intention'],
    ['我不吃饭。', "I don't eat / I'm not going to eat.", 'a habit, or a decision about the future'],
  ]}
  caption="不 before an adjective replaces 很 — 他不忙, never 他不很忙 (which would mean 'not very busy', a different claim)."
/>

## 2. 没 — for 有, and for what didn't happen

<VocabCard hanzi="没" pinyin="méi" meaning="not — for 有, and for actions that didn't happen or haven't happened yet" note="Always méi. Unlike 不, it never changes tone." />

You already know its first job: 有 is only ever negated as 没有. Its second job is the one that
actually earns it a separate word:

<GrammarPoint pattern="我没吃饭。" title="没 denies that something took place">

我没吃饭 — "I didn't eat / I haven't eaten." The claim is that the eating *didn't happen*. Compare
我不吃饭, which says I don't eat (as a habit) or won't eat (as a decision) — a claim about a state
or intention, not about an event failing to occur.

</GrammarPoint>

<Compare
  columns={['', '不', '没']}
  rows={[
    ['denies', 'a state, habit, or intention', 'that an action happened'],
    ['time it points at', 'now, generally, or the future', 'the past, or up to now'],
    ['negates 有?', 'never', 'always — 没有'],
    ['tone', 'bù, or bú before a fourth tone', 'méi, always'],
  ]}
/>

<Predict
  question="You skipped lunch. Which is right for 'I didn't eat'?"
  options={['我不吃饭', '我没吃饭', '我不没吃饭', '我吃饭不']}
  answer={1}
>
  我没吃饭. Lunch was an event that didn't take place, which is exactly what 没 is for. 我不吃饭 would
  mean you don't eat in general, or that you're refusing to — a statement about you, not about
  what happened at noon.
</Predict>

<Predict
  question="'He isn't a teacher' is a statement about what is the case. Which negator?"
  options={['不 — 他不是老师', '没 — 他没是老师', 'Either', 'Neither']}
  answer={0}
>
  不 — 他不是老师. 是 describes a state, and states take 不. 没 would only apply if there were an
  event that failed to happen, and "being a teacher" isn't an event.
</Predict>

## 3. The one sentence that uses both

<GrammarPoint pattern="我不是学生，我没有书。" title="State with 不, possession with 没">

"I'm not a student, and I don't have a book." 不是 because being a student is a state; 没有 because
有 only ever takes 没. Two negators, chosen by rule rather than by feel.

</GrammarPoint>

<Callout kind="why" title="Why this is less arbitrary than it looks">
English speakers tend to feel the 不/没 split as a list of exceptions. It isn't. 不 denies a
description — this is (or isn't) how things are. 没 denies an occurrence — this did (or didn't)
take place. 有 goes with 没 because "having" is treated as something that has or hasn't come
about, not as a description. Once you ask "am I denying a state or an event?", the choice makes
itself.
</Callout>
