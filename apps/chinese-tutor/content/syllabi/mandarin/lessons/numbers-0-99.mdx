---
title: Numbers 0 to 99
summary: Mandarin numbers are almost entirely regular once you learn ten of them.
readingMinutes: 20
---

English has "eleven," "twelve," "thirteen," "twenty," "thirty" — a pile of special forms that have
to be learned one at a time. Mandarin has none of that. Learn 一 through 十 and you can build every
number up to 99 by combination, with no exceptions at all.

## 2. Eleven to nineteen: ten, then the remainder

<GrammarPoint pattern="十 + unit" title="11–19">

十一 = ten-one = 11. 十二 = ten-two = 12. 十五 = 15, 十九 = 19. There is no equivalent of "eleven" or
"twelve" to memorise — the structure is the number.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="Following the pattern, what is 十七?"
  options={['7', '17', '70', '77']}
  answer={1}
>
  17 — ten, then seven. The ten comes first because you're reading it as "ten plus seven."
</Predict>

## 3. Twenty to ninety-nine: multiplier, ten, remainder

<GrammarPoint pattern="unit + 十 + unit" title="20–99">

二十 = two-ten = 20. 三十 = 30. 九十 = 90. Add a remainder on the end and you have everything in
between: 二十一 = two-ten-one = 21, 四十五 = four-ten-five = 45, 九十九 = 99.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="How would you write 68?"
  options={['六八', '八十六', '六十八', '十六八']}
  answer={2}
>
  六十八 — six tens, then eight. (八十六 reverses it into 86, which is the mistake worth watching
  for: the multiplier goes before 十 and the remainder after it.)
</Predict>

<Callout kind="why" title="Why this is worth noticing, not just using">
Research on early arithmetic has repeatedly found that children learning in languages with
transparent number systems like this one tend to grasp place value sooner than English-speaking
children do — because 二十一 says "two tens and a one" out loud, where "twenty-one" hides it. You
get the same benefit as a learner: you are not memorising ninety number words, you are applying
one rule to ten of them.
</Callout>
