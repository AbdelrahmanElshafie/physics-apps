Russian questions need less machinery than English ones. There's no word-order inversion, no
auxiliary verb to insert — a yes/no question is the statement, said differently. The real content
of this lesson is the question words, and the case rules they quietly obey.

## 1. Yes/no questions: change nothing, say it differently

<Compare
  columns={['Russian', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['Ты говоришь по-русски.', 'You speak Russian.'],
    ['Ты говоришь по-русски?', 'Do you speak Russian?'],
  ]}
  caption="Identical words, identical order. Only the rising intonation (and the question mark in writing) marks the question."
/>

<Callout kind="why" title="Why Russian can get away with this">
English has to do something structural — invert ("Are you..."), or summon an auxiliary ("**Do**
you speak...") — because its word order is already carrying grammatical load and can't be left
ambiguous. Russian's grammar is carried by endings, so intonation is free to mark the question
without anything else having to move. This is the same trade-off word order and negation both
turned on.
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="How do you turn Она читает книгу (She is reading a book) into a question?"
  options={['Invert it: Читает она книгу', 'Add an auxiliary verb at the front', 'Leave the words exactly as they are and use rising intonation', 'Move книгу to the front']}
  answer={2}
>
  Leave it alone. Она читает книгу? with rising intonation is the question. (Moving words around
  is allowed, as the word-order lesson showed, but it shifts emphasis — it isn't what makes it a
  question.)
</Predict>

## 2. The question words

<Compare
  columns={['Word', 'Means', 'Example']}
  rows={[
    ['кто', 'who', 'Кто это? — Who is this?'],
    ['что', 'what', 'Что это? — What is this?'],
    ['где', 'where (location)', 'Где книга? — Where is the book?'],
    ['куда', 'where (direction)', 'Куда ты идёшь? — Where are you going?'],
    ['когда', 'when', 'Когда? — When?'],
    ['почему', 'why', 'Почему? — Why?'],
    ['как', 'how', 'Как дела? — How are things?'],
    ['сколько', 'how much / how many', 'Сколько? — How many?'],
  ]}
/>

<VocabCard word="где" stressed="где" meaning="where (being somewhere)" />
<VocabCard word="куда" stressed="куда́" meaning="where to (going somewhere)" />

<Callout kind="warning" title="где vs. куда — English hides a distinction Russian insists on">
English uses "where" for both "Where is the book?" and "Where are you going?" Russian splits them:
**где** asks about a location (and pairs with the prepositional case — где? в доме), **куда** asks
about a destination (and pairs with the accusative — куда? в дом). Using где for a question about
movement is one of the most recognisable beginner mistakes, precisely because English gives no
reason to notice the difference.
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="Which question word belongs in: ___ ты идёшь? (Where are you going?)"
  options={['где', 'куда', 'Either one works', 'когда']}
  answer={1}
>
  куда — the question is about a destination you're moving toward, not a place you're sitting in.
  Где ты идёшь? would be asking which location the walking is happening in, which is a strange
  thing to want to know.
</Predict>

## 3. Question words take cases too

кто and что aren't frozen — they decline like any other word, according to the job they're doing
in the question:

<Compare
  columns={['Form', 'Case', 'Asking']}
  rows={[
    ['Кто читает?', 'nominative', 'Who is reading? (subject)'],
    ['Кого ты видишь?', 'accusative', 'Whom do you see? (object)'],
    ['Кому ты даёшь книгу?', 'dative', 'To whom are you giving the book?'],
  ]}
  caption="The same case logic as any noun — кто is just a noun that happens to mean 'who'. English preserves a faded trace of this in who/whom."
/>

<GrammarPoint pattern="Кому́ ты даёшь кни́гу?" title="Everything from Phase 3 and 4, in one question">

Кому ты даёшь книгу? — "To whom are you giving the book?" кому is dative (the indirect object),
книгу is accusative (the direct object), даёшь is a second-conjugation present-tense verb agreeing
with ты, and the word order needed no inversion at all. That is the whole course so far in one
sentence.

</GrammarPoint>
