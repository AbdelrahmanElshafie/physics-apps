---
title: The genitive case
summary: Possession, negation, and "of" — one case doing three jobs English keeps separate.
readingMinutes: 20
---

English uses three different tools for three different ideas: 's for possession ("the student's
book"), "of" for a relationship between two things ("a glass of water"), and just "not" or "no"
for negation. Russian hands all three of these jobs to a single case: the **genitive**.

## 1. The endings

<Compare
  columns={['Gender', 'Nominative', 'Genitive', 'Rule']}
  rows={[
    ['Masculine (consonant)', 'стол', 'стола', '+ -а'],
    ['Masculine (-й / -ь)', 'чай', 'чая', '+ -я'],
    ['Feminine (-а)', 'книга', 'книги', '-а → -ы (and г blocks ы, so -и)'],
    ['Feminine (-я)', 'семья', 'семьи', '-я → -и'],
    ['Feminine (-ь)', 'дверь', 'двери', '-ь → -и'],
    ['Neuter (-о)', 'окно', 'окна', '-о → -а'],
  ]}
  caption="студент → студента — this is the exact ending animate masculine nouns already borrowed for the accusative, last lesson."
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="Two endings you've already memorized, for free">
книги (genitive of книга) is spelled identically to книги, the nominative *plural* from two
lessons ago — and окна (genitive of окно) is identical to окна, neuter nominative plural. This
isn't a coincidence you need to untangle; Russian genuinely reuses these forms, and context (a
number, "of", a single thing vs. several) tells you which one is meant.
</Callout>

## 2. Possession: whose is it?

<GrammarPoint pattern="Э́то кни́га студе́нта." title="Whose book?">

Это книга студента — "This is the student's book" (literally "This is [a] book of-the-student").
There's no apostrophe-s in Russian; the possessor simply goes into the genitive, right after the
thing possessed.

</GrammarPoint>

  question="You want to say 'the window of the house' (дом, house, masculine). What happens to дом?"

  answer={1}
>
  дома. The possessor/thing being "of"-ed goes into the genitive — masculine consonant-ending

</Predict>

## 3. "Of": describing a relationship between two nouns

<VocabCard word="воды" stressed="воды́" meaning="of water (genitive of вода)" note="стака́н воды́ — a glass of water. вода is feminine, so it follows the regular -а → -ы pattern." />

<GrammarPoint pattern="стака́н воды́" title="A glass of water">

The first noun (стакан, glass) stays nominative — it's the thing that exists. The second noun
(воды, water) goes genitive, because it's describing *what kind of* glass, in relation to the
first.

</GrammarPoint>

## 4. Negation: "there isn't any"

This is the construction most worth memorizing early, because it comes up constantly: **нет** +
genitive means "there is no / there isn't any."

<Dialogue
  caption="Нет + genitive"
  lines={[
    { speaker: 'A', word: 'У тебя есть книга?', stressed: 'У тебя́ есть кни́га?', meaning: 'Do you have a book?' },
    { speaker: 'B', word: 'Нет, у меня нет книги.', stressed: 'Нет, у меня́ нет кни́ги.', meaning: "No, I don't have a book." },
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="кни́га in the question, кни́ги in the answer — not a typo">
Notice the noun itself changed between the question and the answer. есть ("there is/exists") takes
a normal nominative subject — кни́га. нет ("there isn't") is grammatically the negation of "there
is," and it specifically demands the genitive — кни́ги. This swap (nominative after "есть", genitive
after "нет") is completely regular and applies to every noun, not just книга.
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="У меня нет ___ (стол, table, masculine inanimate). What form does стол take after нет?"
  options={['стол (unchanged)', 'стола — genitive', 'столу', 'столе']}
  answer={1}
>
  стола. нет always takes the genitive, regardless of whether the noun is animate or inanimate —
  unlike the accusative, genitive doesn't split by animacy.
</Predict>
