Twenty words, and most of the larger numbers are built out of them. But the genuinely interesting
part of this topic isn't the counting — it's what happens to the *noun* when you put a number in
front of it, which is unlike anything English does.

## 2. Eleven to twenty: one pattern, one irregular stress

The teens are transparently built: take the unit, add **-надцать** (a worn-down form of an old
phrase meaning "on ten").

<Compare
  columns={['Number', 'Russian', 'Built from']}
  rows={[
    ['11', 'одиннадцать', 'один + надцать'],
    ['12', 'двенадцать', 'две + надцать'],
    ['13', 'тринадцать', 'три + надцать'],
    ['14', 'четырнадцать', 'четыр(е) + надцать'],
    ['15', 'пятнадцать', 'пять + надцать'],
    ['16', 'шестнадцать', 'шесть + надцать'],
    ['17', 'семнадцать', 'семь + надцать'],
    ['18', 'восемнадцать', 'восемь + надцать'],
    ['19', 'девятнадцать', 'девять + надцать'],
    ['20', 'двадцать', 'два + дцать (a different, parallel formation)'],
  ]}
  caption="Recognizing -надцать means you only ever had to learn the units — the teens come almost free."
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="четы́рнадцать is the odd one out for stress">
Almost every teen puts the stress on -на́дцать: пятна́дцать, шестна́дцать, семна́дцать,
восемна́дцать, девятна́дцать, and even оди́ннадцать and двена́дцать stress the syllable before it.
**четы́рнадцать** keeps its stress all the way back on четы́-, where четы́ре had it. It's the one
teen worth drilling separately.
</Callout>

## 3. The real content: a number changes the noun's case

In English, a number leaves the noun alone except for plural -s: "one table, two tables, five
tables." Russian splits numbers into three groups, each putting the following noun in a different
case:

<Compare
  columns={['Number', 'Noun goes into', 'Example']}
  rows={[
    ['1', 'nominative singular (and один agrees in gender)', 'один стол · одна книга · одно окно'],
    ['2, 3, 4', 'genitive singular', 'два стола́ · три кни́ги · четыре окна́'],
    ['5–20', 'genitive plural', 'пять столо́в · десять книг · двадцать о́кон'],
  ]}
  caption="This is why the genitive lessons came first — the numbers topic can't be taught without them."
/>

<GrammarPoint pattern="оди́н стол · два стола́ · пять столо́в" title="One noun, three forms, decided by the number">

Nothing about стол changed its meaning across these three — only the number in front of it
decided which case it had to appear in. Memorizing the three brackets (1 / 2–4 / 5 and up) is the
whole rule.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="You want to say 'three books' (книга, feminine). What form does книга take?"
  options={['книга — nominative, unchanged', 'книги — genitive singular, because 3 is in the 2–4 bracket', 'книг — genitive plural', 'книгу — accusative']}
  answer={1}
>
  кни́ги — genitive singular, since 3 falls in the 2–4 bracket. The fact that "three books" is
  plural in English is irrelevant; Russian is following its own bracket rule, not English's
  singular/plural logic.
</Predict>

## 4. один and два agree in gender; the rest don't

Two of the numbers change form depending on the noun's gender:

<Compare
  columns={['Gender', 'one', 'two']}
  rows={[
    ['masculine', 'один стол', 'два стола́'],
    ['feminine', 'одна книга', 'две кни́ги'],
    ['neuter', 'одно окно', 'два окна́'],
  ]}
  caption="два vs. две is the split worth remembering: две goes with feminine nouns, два with masculine and neuter. From три upward, the number itself never changes for gender."
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="четы́ре окна́ vs. о́кна — same letters, different grammar">
The nominative-case lesson taught о́кна as the *plural* of окно, stressed on the first syllable.
Here, четыре окна́ is the *genitive singular*, stressed on the last. Identical spelling, different
stress, completely different grammatical job — a good illustration of why this course marks stress
on everything it prints.
</Callout>
