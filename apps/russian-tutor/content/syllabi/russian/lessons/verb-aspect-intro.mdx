---
title: 'Verb aspect: perfective vs. imperfective'
summary: Whether an action is ongoing or completed changes the verb itself, not just a tense marker.
readingMinutes: 25
---

English marks "ongoing" vs. "finished" with helper words and endings: "I was reading" vs. "I
read [it]," "I am writing" vs. "I wrote." Russian marks the same distinction with a completely
different *verb* — not a different ending on the same verb. This is called **aspect**, and almost
every Russian verb you learn comes as a pair.

## 1. Two verbs, one meaning, different focus

<Compare
  columns={['Aspect', 'Verb', 'Focus']}
  rows={[
    ['Imperfective', 'читать', 'The process — ongoing, repeated, or habitual reading'],
    ['Perfective', 'прочитать', 'The result — reading all the way through to completion'],
  ]}
  caption={'Both verbs translate to English "read" — the difference is entirely about whether the action is viewed as a process or as a completed whole.'}
/>

<GrammarPoint pattern="чита́ть  →  прочита́ть" title="Imperfective → perfective, by prefix">

Adding the prefix про- turns the imperfective читать into its perfective partner прочитать. This
is the single most common way aspect pairs are formed — a prefix added to the imperfective verb.
делать ("to do/make") → сделать works the same way, with the prefix с- instead.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="Я читал книгу всё утро (I was reading a book all morning) describes an ongoing process. Which aspect is читал?"
  options={['Perfective', 'Imperfective', 'Neither — aspect only applies to the present tense', "It's not possible to tell"]}
  answer={1}
>
  Imperfective. "All morning" describes a stretch of ongoing activity, with no claim that the book
  was finished — exactly the kind of meaning imperfective verbs carry.
</Predict>

## 2. The structural rule that makes this more than vocabulary

Here is the part that makes aspect a grammar topic, not just a pair of synonyms: **perfective
verbs have no present tense at all.**

<Callout kind="warning" title="A completed action can't be happening right now">
Think about why: the present tense describes something happening *right now*, in progress. But
"perfective" specifically means viewed as a single, completed whole — and a completed action,
by definition, isn't still in progress. "I am completing the book" the instant it's finished is a
contradiction, not a sentence. So perfective verbs skip the present tense entirely and only have
past and future forms.
</Callout>

<GrammarPoint pattern="я прочита́ю (future)  ·  я прочита́л (past)  ·  — (no present)" title="прочитать has no present tense">

прочитать conjugates for the future (я прочитаю — "I will [completely] read") and the past (я
прочитал — "I read [finished] it"), but there is no "я прочитаю" meaning "I am completely-reading"
right now — that meaning doesn't exist in any language's present tense, Russian just makes the gap
structurally explicit by giving the verb no present-tense slot to fill.
</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="Every verb conjugated back in the present-tense-conjugation lesson (читаю, говоришь, говорят...) — what aspect must they all be, given that they all have present-tense forms?"
  options={['Perfective', 'Imperfective', 'It varies verb by verb', 'Aspect only exists in the past tense']}
  answer={1}
>
  Imperfective. Since perfective verbs structurally cannot have a present tense, any verb you see
  correctly conjugated in the present tense is, by that fact alone, imperfective.
</Predict>

## 3. What the future of a perfective verb actually means

A perfective future (я прочитаю) is conjugated exactly like an imperfective present — same
endings, same two conjugation patterns from the last lesson — but it means something different: a
single action that *will be completed*, not an ongoing state.

<Compare

  rows={[

  ]}
  caption="The imperfective future needs an extra helper verb (буду); the perfective future doesn't — it conjugates directly, the same shape as an imperfective present. Both are covered properly in the future-tense lesson."
