title: Negation
summary: "Не before the verb, and the double-negative rule that English doesn't have."
readingMinutes: 15
---

Negation in Russian is mechanically simpler than English — there's no "do" to conjure up — but it
has one rule that looks, to an English speaker, like a mistake: you often need *several* negatives
in the same sentence, and leaving any of them out is what's actually wrong.

## 1. не goes in front of whatever is being negated

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я не чита́ю." title="не + verb">

Я не читаю — "I don't read / I'm not reading." No auxiliary, no change to the verb itself: не
simply sits in front of it. Compare the work English does here — "I read" has to become "I **do**
not read," summoning a verb that wasn't in the positive sentence at all.

</GrammarPoint>

<VocabCard word="не" stressed="не" meaning="not (the negation particle)" note="Unstressed — it leans on the word after it, which is why it never carries its own accent mark in this course's lesson text." />

не can negate something other than the verb, if that's what you mean to deny — it goes directly
before whichever word is being contradicted:

<Compare
  columns={['Russian', 'Means']}
  rows={[
    ['Я не читаю книгу.', "I'm not reading a book (denying the reading)"],
    ['Я читаю не книгу.', "It's not a book I'm reading (denying that it's a book — a newspaper, maybe)"],
  ]}
  caption="Same words, different placement of не, different thing being denied."
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="не (particle) vs. нет (the word 'no')">
These are two different words. **не** is the particle that negates something inside a sentence
(Я не читаю). **нет** is the standalone answer "no" — and, separately, the "there isn't any"
word that takes the genitive, from the genitive-case lesson (У меня нет книги). Answering a
question with "не" alone, or sticking "нет" in front of a verb, are both wrong.
</Callout>

## 2. Double negatives are required, not forbidden

In English, "I don't know nothing" is either dialect or a logic puzzle, because two negatives
are taken to cancel. In Russian, the negative words **require** не on the verb as well — leaving
it out is a grammatical error:

<Compare
  columns={['Negative word', 'Meaning', 'Example']}
  rows={[
    ['ничего', 'nothing', 'Я ничего не знаю. — I know nothing.'],
    ['никто', 'nobody', 'Никто не знает. — Nobody knows.'],
    ['никогда', 'never', 'Я никогда не был в Москве. — I have never been to Moscow.'],
    ['нигде', 'nowhere', 'Я нигде не был. — I have been nowhere.'],
  ]}
  caption="Every one of these needs the не on the verb too. Я ничего знаю is not a sentence."
/>

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я ничего́ не зна́ю." title="Literally: I nothing not know">

Я ничего не знаю — "I don't know anything." Two negatives (ничего and не), and both are
obligatory. They don't cancel each other out; in Russian they agree with each other, the same way
an adjective agrees with its noun.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="How would you say 'Nobody knows' — Никто знает, or Никто не знает?"
  options={['Никто знает — one negative is enough', 'Никто не знает — the verb needs не as well', 'Both are correct', 'Neither — Russian has no word for nobody']}
  answer={1}
>
  Никто не знает. The negative pronoun никто does not excuse the verb from carrying не — Russian
  negation spreads across the sentence rather than being used up in one place.
</Predict>

<Callout kind="why" title="Why this isn't illogical">
It's tempting to read Russian double negation as "two negatives that should cancel." A better
frame: Russian marks negation on every element that participates in it, the way it marks case on
every noun and gender on every past-tense verb. Agreement, not arithmetic — the sentence is
negative once, and every word in it that can show that fact does.
</Callout>
