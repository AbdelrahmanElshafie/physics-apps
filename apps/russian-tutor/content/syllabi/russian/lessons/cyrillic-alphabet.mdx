---
title: The Cyrillic alphabet
summary: Most letters aren't as foreign as they look — a handful of true look-alikes and false friends cover almost everything new.
readingMinutes: 12
---

Cyrillic looks intimidating from a distance, but most of that is an illusion: a third of the
alphabet looks and sounds just like its Latin counterpart, another chunk looks familiar but
sounds completely different, and only about a third is genuinely new shapes. Learning it is
sorting letters into those three piles, not memorizing thirty-three mysteries from scratch.

## 1. True friends — look the same, sound the same

These five read exactly the way an English speaker would guess:

## 2. False friends — look familiar, sound nothing alike

This is the part worth actually slowing down for. These letters borrow a Latin shape but carry a
completely different sound — reading them as if they were English is the single most common
mistake a beginner makes:

<Compare
  columns={['Letter', 'Looks like', 'Actually sounds like']}
  rows={[
    ['В в', 'Latin B', '"v" in "van"'],
    ['Е е', 'Latin E', '"ye" in "yes"'],
    ['Н н', 'Latin H', '"n" in "net"'],
    ['Р р', 'Latin P', '"r" (rolled, like Spanish)'],
    ['С с', 'Latin C', '"s" in "sun"'],
    ['У у', 'Latin Y', '"oo" in "boot"'],
    ['Х х', 'Latin X', '"ch" in Scottish "loch"'],
  ]}
  caption="Seven letters, seven false friends — worth drilling on their own before anything else."
/>

<Predict
  question="РЕСТОРАН — read each letter by what it actually sounds like, not what it looks like. What word is this?"
  options={['A made-up word', 'RESTORAN — "restaurant"', 'A number', "Someone's name"]}

>
  Р = R, Е = YE... but after a consonant Е softens to just "e", С = S, Т = T, О = O, Р = R, А = A,
  Н = N — RESTAURANT. Every letter here was either a true friend or a false friend from the two
  tables above; nothing new. This is exactly why the false-friend list matters more than it looks
  like it should: get those seven right and whole words click into place immediately.
</Predict>

## 3. New shapes — genuinely new letters

The rest of the alphabet has no Latin shape to borrow or misread, so there's nothing to unlearn —
these are just new:

<Compare
  columns={['Letter', 'Sounds like']}
  rows={[
    ['Б б', '"b" in "bat"'],
    ['Г г', '"g" in "go"'],
    ['Д д', '"d" in "dog"'],
    ['Ж ж', '"s" in "pleasure"'],
    ['З з', '"z" in "zoo"'],
    ['И и', '"ee" in "see"'],
    ['Л л', '"l" in "lamp"'],
    ['П п', '"p" in "pot"'],
    ['Ф ф', '"f" in "fun"'],
    ['Ц ц', '"ts" in "cats"'],
    ['Ч ч', '"ch" in "chip"'],
    ['Ш ш', '"sh" in "shop"'],
    ['Щ щ', '"shch" — a longer, softer "sh"'],
    ['Ы ы', 'no English equivalent — between "i" and "u"'],
    ['Э э', '"e" in "met"'],
    ['Ю ю', '"you"'],
    ['Я я', '"ya" in "yard"'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="Ь and Ъ carry no sound of their own">
Two more symbols round out the alphabet: **ь** (the "soft sign") softens the consonant right
before it, and **ъ** (the "hard sign") does the opposite, keeping a consonant and the vowel after
it from blending. Neither is pronounced on its own — they're pronunciation instructions for their
neighbors, not sounds.
</Callout>
