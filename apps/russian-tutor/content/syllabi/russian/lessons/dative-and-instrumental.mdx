Two more cases, covered together because each is a single, clean idea: the **dative** marks "to"
or "for" someone, and the **instrumental** marks "by means of" or "with" something.

## 1. The dative: to / for

    ['Masculine (-й / -ь)', 'чай', 'чаю', '+ -ю'],
    ['Feminine (-а / -я)', 'книга', 'книге', '→ -е'],
    ['Feminine (-ь)', 'дверь', 'двери', '→ -и'],
    ['Neuter (-о)', 'окно', 'окну', '→ -у'],
  ]}
/>

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я даю́ кни́гу студе́нту." title="The indirect object">

Я даю книгу студенту — "I give the book to the student." книгу is the direct object
(accusative, from two lessons ago); студенту is the indirect object — *to* whom the giving
happens — in the dative.

</GrammarPoint>

### The construction you'll use constantly: нра́виться ("to be pleasing to")

Russian doesn't have a verb that works exactly like "to like." Instead, it says something is
*pleasing to* someone — and "someone" is in the dative:

<VocabCard word="мне" stressed="мне" meaning="to me (dative)" />
<VocabCard word="тебе" stressed="тебе́" meaning="to you, informal (dative)" />

<Dialogue
  caption="Мне нравится... (literally: to-me is-pleasing...)"
  lines={[
    { speaker: '', word: 'Мне нравится книга.', stressed: 'Мне нра́вится кни́га.', meaning: 'I like the book. (lit. "To me, the book is pleasing.")' },
    { speaker: '', word: 'Тебе нравится чай?', stressed: 'Тебе́ нра́вится чай?', meaning: 'Do you like tea? (lit. "Is tea pleasing to you?")' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="The liked thing is the grammatical subject, not the object">
This trips up almost every English speaker at first: in "I like the book," English makes "I" the
subject. In Мне нравится книга, книга — the thing being liked — is the nominative subject (that's
why it's книга, not книгу), and мне (the person doing the liking) is in the dative. The verb
нравится agrees with книга, not with "I."
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="You want to say 'The student likes tea' — Студенту нравится ___. What form should чай take?"
  options={['чай — nominative, unchanged', 'чая — genitive', 'чаю — dative', 'чаем — instrumental']}
  answer={0}
>
  чай, unchanged. чай is the thing being liked — the grammatical subject of нравится — so it stays
  nominative, exactly like книга did. Студенту (the one doing the liking) is what's in the dative
  here.
</Predict>

## 2. The instrumental: by means of / with

    ['Feminine (-я)', 'семья', 'семьёй', '→ -ёй'],
    ['Feminine (-ь)', 'дверь', 'дверью', '→ -ью'],
    ['Neuter (-о)', 'окно', 'окном', '→ -ом'],
  ]}
/>

<VocabCard word="ручкой" stressed="ру́чкой" meaning="with a pen (instrumental of ручка)" />

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я пишу́ ру́чкой." title="The tool you do something with — no preposition needed">

Я пишу ручкой — "I write with a pen." English needs the word "with"; Russian just puts ручка into
the instrumental. No preposition at all is required when the instrumental marks a tool or means.

</GrammarPoint>

### с + instrumental: accompaniment ("together with")

When "with" means accompanying a *person*, rather than using a tool, Russian adds the preposition
**с** in front of the instrumental:

<VocabCard word="другом" stressed="дру́гом" meaning="with a friend (с + instrumental of друг)" />

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я иду́ с дру́гом." title="с + instrumental = together with">

Я иду с другом — "I'm going with a friend." Drop the с and this would read as "I'm going
[by means of] a friend," which makes no sense for a person — с specifically marks "alongside,"
while the bare instrumental (no preposition) marks "using as a tool."

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="Я пишу ___ (ручка, pen) vs. Я иду с ___ (друг, friend) — why does one need с and the other doesn't?"
  options={['No real difference — с is optional either way', 'A tool takes bare instrumental; a person accompanying you needs с + instrumental', "It's random, just memorize both", 'с is required whenever the noun is masculine']}
  answer={1}
>
  A tool (ручкой) takes the bare instrumental with no preposition; accompanying a person (с
  другом) needs с in front of it. The case ending is instrumental either way — с is what changes
  "by means of" into "together with."
</Predict>
