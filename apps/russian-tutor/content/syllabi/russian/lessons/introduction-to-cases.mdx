---
title: Why Russian has cases
summary: A noun's ending changes with its job in the sentence — what English does with word order, Russian does with endings.
readingMinutes: 20
---

English shows what role a noun plays in a sentence almost entirely through **word order**. "The
dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" use the exact same words — only the order tells you
who did the biting. Russian solves the same problem a completely different way: the noun itself
changes its ending depending on its job in the sentence. This system is called **cases**.

<Callout kind="why" title="The one idea this whole phase is built on">
Every noun you've learned so far (стол, книга, окно) has been sitting in its **dictionary
form** — the form you'd look it up under. That dictionary form is itself a case (the nominative,
next lesson) — not a case-free "neutral" state. There is no case-free version of a Russian noun;
every noun in every sentence is always in some case. Phase 3 is six lessons, each teaching what
one case is for and what it does to a word's ending.
</Callout>

## 2. One ending change, many possible jobs

A case isn't just "marks the object." Each of the six Russian cases answers a different question
about a noun's role:

/>

<Callout kind="note" title="You already produce one case correctly, every time">
Every VocabCard you've seen so far has shown a noun's **nominative** form — its dictionary form,
the one used for the subject of a sentence. You haven't been avoiding cases up to now; you've
been using exactly one of them (correctly) the whole time, because every new noun is naturally
introduced as a subject first.
</Callout>

## 3. The ending depends on gender — this is why gender came first

The same case produces a *different* ending depending on whether the noun is masculine, feminine,
or neuter — which is exactly why the previous lesson on gender had to come before this one.

<GrammarPoint pattern="сто́л → стол + а  ·  кни́га → книг + и  ·  окно́ → окн + а́" title="A preview: the genitive ending, three ways">

These are the genitive ("of") forms of стол (masculine), книга (feminine), and окно (neuter) —
*stóla*, *knígi*, *okná*. Three different endings for the exact same case, because the ending
pattern depends on the noun's gender. This is the shape every case lesson from here on takes:
one case, three gender-specific endings to learn together.

</GrammarPoint>

## 4. Why bother — isn't word order enough?

It's worth being honest about the trade-off: cases are more to learn up front, but they let
Russian do things English structurally can't without extra words. "Я дал кни́гу сестре́" (I gave
the book to-sister) packs subject, direct object, *and* indirect object into word endings alone —
English needs "to" to do the job дательный падеж (the dative case, Module 3.2) does with a single
letter change.
