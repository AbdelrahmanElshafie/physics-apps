---
title: The accusative case
summary: Marking the direct object — what the verb is actually acting on.
readingMinutes: 20
---

The accusative marks the **direct object** — the thing a verb is actually doing something to.
"I read **a book**", "I see **the table**" — in English, "book" and "table" are only object
because of where they sit in the sentence. In Russian, the noun itself changes.

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я чита́ю кни́гу." title="Subject unchanged, object changed">

Я читаю книгу — "I read a/the book." книга (nominative, the dictionary form) becomes книгу the
moment it's the thing being read. Я ("I") doesn't change — pronouns have their own, separate set
of forms, covered later. This lesson is about what happens to *nouns*.

</GrammarPoint>

## 1. Feminine nouns: the clearest change

This is the easiest pattern to spot, so it's worth anchoring on first:

<Compare
  columns={['Nominative', 'Accusative', 'Rule']}
  rows={[
    ['книга', 'книгу', '-а → -у'],
    ['семья', 'семью', '-я → -ю'],
  ]}
  caption="Every feminine noun ending in -а or -я follows this pattern, no exceptions worth learning yet."
/>

<VocabCard word="газету" stressed="газе́ту" meaning="newspaper (accusative) — from газета" />

<Predict
  question="студентка (female student, feminine, ends in -а) is the object of 'I see the student.' What form does it take?"
  options={['студентка (unchanged)', 'студентку', 'студентки', 'студентке']}
  answer={1}
>
  студентку — the regular -а → -у pattern. (Note this isn't the 7-letter spelling rule from the
  last lesson; that rule is about ы specifically, and у is unaffected by it.)
</Predict>

## 2. Masculine nouns: it depends on whether the noun is alive

This is the part that makes the accusative genuinely tricky, and it's worth sitting with: for
masculine nouns, the accusative ending depends on **animacy** — whether the noun refers to
something alive.

<Compare
  columns={['Type', 'Accusative rule', 'Example']}
  rows={[
    ['Masculine, inanimate (things)', 'Same as nominative — no change', 'Я вижу стол. (I see the table.)'],
    ['Masculine, animate (people, animals)', 'Same as genitive — adds -а', 'Я вижу студента. (I see the student.)'],
  ]}
  caption="стол stays стол; студент becomes студента — not студент, even though both are masculine."
/>

<Callout kind="warning" title="This is genuinely two different rules wearing one name">
It feels strange that "the accusative case" means two completely different things depending on
whether the noun is alive — but that's exactly what's happening, and it isn't a simplification
this course is skipping past. The genitive case (Module 3.2, right after this one) is what
actually produces that -а ending for студент → студента; animate-masculine accusative nouns are
borrowing the genitive's form rather than having a true ending of their own.
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="соба́ка (dog) in Russian grammar counts as animate. If мужчи́на (man, also animate) takes -а in the accusative like студент does, what do you expect for a masculine animate noun in general?"
  options={['It stays unchanged, like inanimate masculine nouns', 'It takes the genitive-style -а ending, same pattern as студента', 'It becomes feminine', 'There is no rule — every animate noun is different']}
  answer={1}
>
  It takes the genitive-style ending. Any masculine noun referring to a living thing — person or
  animal — follows the студент → студента pattern, not the "stays the same" pattern inanimate
  masculine nouns use.
</Predict>

## 3. Neuter nouns: unchanged, just like inanimate masculine

Neuter nouns in the accusative are identical to their nominative form — no ending change at all,
for the same reason stол doesn't change: the overwhelming majority of neuter nouns are things, not
living beings.

<VocabCard word="окно" stressed="окно́" meaning="window — same in nominative and accusative" />

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я ви́жу окно́." title="Neuter: nominative = accusative">

Я вижу окно — "I see the window." окно is identical whether it's the subject or the object; only
context (word order, or the verb) tells you which job it's doing here.

</GrammarPoint>
