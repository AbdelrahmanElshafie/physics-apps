---
title: Noun gender
summary: Every Russian noun is masculine, feminine, or neuter — and the ending almost always gives it away.
readingMinutes: 20
---

English nouns don't have grammatical gender — "table" isn't masculine or feminine, it's just a
table. Russian nouns all have one of three genders: **masculine**, **feminine**, or **neuter**.
This isn't about the thing itself being inherently male, female, or neither — a table (стол) is
"masculine" the same arbitrary way a French table (*la table*) is "feminine". It's a grammatical
category, not a statement about the object.

<Callout kind="why" title="Why this matters before anything else">
Gender isn't trivia. Adjectives change their ending to match the noun they describe, pronouns
("it" is он/она/оно depending on gender), and — much later — the past tense of verbs agrees with
gender too. Get the gender of a noun wrong and several other words in the sentence become wrong
along with it. This is why it comes before cases: every case ending you'll learn next also
depends on which gender the noun is.
</Callout>

## 1. The ending usually tells you

For the large majority of nouns, you can read the gender straight off the last letter of the
dictionary form:

## 2. Soft-sign nouns (-ь): the one real ambiguity

Nouns ending in the soft sign **ь** can be *either* masculine or feminine — the spelling alone
doesn't tell you, unlike every other ending above. There's no shortcut here: these have to be
learned noun by noun, the same way an English speaker just has to know "sheep" is both singular
and plural.

<Compare
  columns={['Word', 'Gender', 'Meaning']}
  rows={[
    ['день', 'masculine', 'day'],
    ['конь', 'masculine', 'horse'],
    ['дверь', 'feminine', 'door'],
    ['ночь', 'feminine', 'night'],
  ]}
  caption="Same ending, both genders — this is the one place you memorize the word, not the rule."
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="A pattern that helps, but isn't a rule">
Most feminine ь-nouns describe abstract or soft concepts and end in a consonant cluster right
before the ь (ночь, дверь), while most masculine ь-nouns end in a single consonant + ь after a
vowel (день, конь). This is a *tendency*, not a rule you can rely on — treat soft-sign nouns as
vocabulary to memorize with their gender attached, the way you'd memorize a word's stress.
</Callout>

## 3. When natural gender overrides the ending

A small set of common words describing people break the ending rule on purpose: they end in
**-а** or **-я** like a feminine noun, but refer to a male person, and are grammatically
**masculine** because of it. Natural gender (is this person male or female?) wins over the
spelling rule.

<VocabCard word="мужчина" stressed="мужчи́на" meaning="man (masculine, despite the -а ending)" note={'Refers to a male person, so it takes masculine adjectives and verb agreement — "big man" is большо́й мужчи́на, not the feminine form.'} />
<VocabCard word="папа" stressed="па́па" meaning="dad (masculine, despite the -а ending)" />
<VocabCard word="дядя" stressed="дя́дя" meaning="uncle (masculine, despite the -я ending)" />

  a man, so it's masculine despite looking like a typical feminine noun. This isn't random: every

## 4. Indeclinable loanwords: always neuter, no exceptions

A handful of borrowed words — mostly from French — never change their ending for any case, and by
convention are always treated as **neuter**, regardless of what their ending looks like:
