---
title: The prepositional case
summary: The case that only ever shows up after a preposition — true to its name.
readingMinutes: 15
---

The last of the six cases is also the simplest to recognize: the **prepositional** never appears
on its own. It only shows up after specific prepositions — mainly **в** ("in"), **на** ("on"),
and **о** ("about") — which is exactly where its name comes from.

## 1. The endings

<Compare
  columns={['Gender', 'Nominative', 'Prepositional', 'Rule']}
  rows={[
    ['Masculine (consonant)', 'стол', 'столе', '+ -е'],
    ['Masculine (-й)', 'музей', 'музее', '-й → -е'],
    ['Feminine (-а / -я)', 'книга', 'книге', '→ -е'],
    ['Feminine (-ь)', 'дверь', 'двери', '→ -и'],
    ['Neuter (-о)', 'окно', 'окне', '→ -е'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="книге again — the third case sharing this exact form">
книге is now the dative form (last lesson) *and* the prepositional form. Feminine -а/-я nouns use
-е for both cases; дверь likewise uses -и for genitive, dative, *and* prepositional. Russian
reuses a small set of endings across several cases — recognizing that a form is "one of the -е/-и
endings" matters more day to day than being able to name which single case produced it.
</Callout>

## 2. в / на + prepositional: location

<GrammarPoint pattern="Кни́га в до́ме." title="в + prepositional = in">

Книга в доме — "The book is in the house." до́м (house) → до́ме after в.

</GrammarPoint>

<GrammarPoint pattern="Кни́га на столе́." title="на + prepositional = on">

Книга на столе — "The book is on the table." стол → столе after на.

</GrammarPoint>

<Callout kind="warning" title="в vs. на isn't always literal 'inside' vs. 'on top of'">
The general pattern — в for enclosed spaces, на for surfaces or open events — holds most of the
time, but a handful of common nouns are simply idiomatic exceptions that have to be learned with
the preposition attached: **на работе** ("at work") and **на уро́ке** ("in class/at the lesson")
both use на despite not being literal surfaces. Learn these as fixed phrases rather than trying to
derive them from the rule.
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="Студент в до́ме. If you wanted to say the student is instead sitting on a chair (стул, masculine), which preposition would you expect?"
  options={['в стуле', 'на стуле', 'о стуле', 'стуле with no preposition']}
  answer={1}
>
  на стуле — a chair is a surface you sit on top of, so на fits the general pattern (unlike the
  на работе-style exceptions, which just have to be memorized).
</Predict>

## 3. о + prepositional: about

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я ду́маю о кни́ге." title="о + prepositional = about">

Я думаю о книге — "I'm thinking about the book." книга → книге after о — the same -е ending the
dative already uses, but a completely different meaning here, supplied entirely by о.

</GrammarPoint>

<Predict
  question="Preposition о always triggers which case?"
  options={['Accusative', 'Genitive', 'Prepositional', 'It varies by verb']}
  answer={2}
>
  Prepositional — that's exactly why it has this name. Every preposition in Russian is actually
  tied to one or more specific cases; о happens to always take the prepositional.
</Predict>
