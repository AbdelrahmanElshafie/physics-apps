---
title: Saying who you are
summary: Names, nationality, and the verb "to be" — which Russian simply drops in the present tense.
readingMinutes: 20
---

With greetings covered, the next thing any conversation needs is saying who you are — your name,
where you're from, what you do. Along the way, this lesson introduces the single most distinctive
fact about Russian grammar in the present tense: there often isn't a verb at all.

## 1. Giving your name

There are two ways to give your name, and the more common one isn't the one you'd guess from
English:

<VocabCard word="Меня зовут" stressed="Меня́ зову́т" meaning={'My name is... (lit. "[they] call me")'} note="Literally 'they call me' — an impersonal construction, not 'I am called.' This is the everyday, natural way to give your name." />

<Dialogue
  caption="Introducing yourself"
  lines={[
    { speaker: 'A', word: 'Как тебя зовут?', stressed: 'Как тебя́ зову́т?', meaning: 'What is your name? (lit. "How do they call you?")' },
    { speaker: 'B', word: 'Меня зовут Анна.', stressed: 'Меня́ зову́т А́нна.', meaning: 'My name is Anna.' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="note" title="Я ... also works, but зовут is more natural">
Я Анна ("I [am] Anna") is understandable and not wrong, but Меня зовут Анна is what a native
speaker actually says far more often — the same way "I'm called Anna" sounds slightly odd to an
English ear next to the far more natural "I'm Anna," except in Russian the roles are reversed.
</Callout>

## 2. Nationality: an adjective that agrees with you

Saying your nationality uses an adjective — and like any Russian adjective, it has to agree in
gender with the person it describes:

  rows={[
    ['A man says:', 'Я русский.', "I'm Russian. (masculine form)"],
    ['A woman says:', 'Я русская.', "I'm Russian. (feminine form)"],
    ['A man says:', 'Я американец.', "I'm American. (masculine form)"],
    ['A woman says:', 'Я американка.', "I'm American. (feminine form)"],
  ]}
  caption="Notice there's no verb anywhere in any of these four sentences — more on that below."
/>

<Predict
  question="A woman wants to say she's Russian. Which form does she use?"

>
  Я русская — the nationality adjective has to agree with the speaker's own gender, exactly the
  way any adjective agrees with the noun it describes.
</Predict>

## 3. Where you're from: из + genitive

<VocabCard word="откуда" stressed="отку́да" meaning="from where" />
<VocabCard word="России" stressed="Росси́и" meaning="from Russia (genitive of Россия)" />

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я из Росси́и." title="из + genitive = from">

Я из России — "I'm from Russia." из ("from," like о and в/на) governs the genitive case — this is
exactly the pattern Module 3.2 already taught, just with a new preposition attached to it.

</GrammarPoint>

## 4. The big point: no "to be" in the present tense

Look back at every sentence in this lesson: Я русский, Я американка, Я из России. None of them
contain a word for "am." This isn't a coincidence or something left out of this course for
simplicity — Russian genuinely has **no present-tense form of "to be"** in ordinary speech. The
subject and the description just sit next to each other.

<GrammarPoint pattern="Я врач." title="Subject + description, no verb needed">

Я врач — "I am a doctor." There's no word doing the job of "am" here at all; Russian just states
the two things (я, врач) side by side and the meaning is complete.

</GrammarPoint>

<Callout kind="warning" title={'This is present tense only — быть ("to be") does exist'}>
Russian does have a verb "to be" (быть), and you'll meet it soon — it's simply skipped in the
present tense specifically. The past tense ("I was a doctor") and future tense ("I will be a
doctor") both do use a form of быть; only "am/is/are" vanishes.
</Callout>

<Predict
  question="Given that 'to be' only vanishes in the present tense, would you expect 'Я была врач' (I was a doctor) to include a verb?"
  options={['No — Russian never uses "to be" in any tense', 'Yes — была is a past-tense form of быть, present tense is the only one that drops it', 'Only questions use "to be"', "It depends on nationality"]}
  answer={1}
>
  Yes. была is exactly a past-tense form of быть ("to be") — the zero-copula rule is specific to
  the present tense; past and future both use a real verb.
</Predict>
