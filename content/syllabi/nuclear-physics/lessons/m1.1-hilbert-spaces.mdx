<Callout kind="why">
A vector space lets you add states and scale them. It does **not** let you ask the one question
physics always asks: *how much of this state is that one?* That question is the inner product,
and its answer squared is a probability. Everything from orthogonal spin states to a scattering
amplitude is this one operation.
</Callout>

## 1. What a vector space is missing

From the last lesson, a vector space gives you exactly two operations: add two vectors, and
scale one by a number. That is enough to write a superposition — but notice what you still
cannot do.

<Compare
  caption="What each structure buys you"
  columns={['Question', 'Vector space alone', 'With an inner product']}
  rows={[
    ['Can I add two states?', 'yes', 'yes'],
    ['How long is this vector?', 'no notion of length', '$\\lVert v \\rVert$'],
    ['What is the angle between them?', 'no notion of angle', '$\\cos\\theta$'],
    ['Are these two states distinguishable?', 'cannot ask', 'orthogonality'],
    ['How much of $v$ points along $u$?', 'cannot ask', 'the projection'],
  ]}
/>

Length and angle are not decoration. Without them there is no normalisation, so no probability —
and quantum mechanics is a theory of probabilities.

## 2. The inner product, starting from the dot product

You already know one inner product: the dot product in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

<Eq
  id="dot-product"
  label="Dot product in R-two"
  latex={String.raw`\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2`}
/>

<Derivation caption="A concrete one">
  <Step
    why="Multiply matching components."
    latex={String.raw`(3, 2) \cdot (1, 4) = (3)(1) + (2)(4)`}
  />
  <Step why="Add them. The result is a single number, not a vector." latex={String.raw`= 3 + 8 = 11`} />
</Derivation>

Drag the two arrows below. Watch the number, and try to make it zero.

  caption="The inner product of two real vectors, with the projection of v onto u shown in green."
/>

<Callout kind="note">
Three things worth noticing while you drag. The product is **largest** when the arrows point the

opposite. It measures agreement of direction, weighted by length.
</Callout>

## 3. Why complex numbers force a change

Quantum states live in <Symbol name="complex">complex</Symbol> spaces, and the naive formula
breaks immediately. Take $v = (i, 0)$ and compute its length the obvious way:

<Eq
  id="naive-complex"
  label="The naive attempt fails"
  latex={String.raw`v_1 v_1 + v_2 v_2 = (i)(i) + 0 = i^2 = -1`}
/>

A length of $-1$ is meaningless. The fix is to conjugate the first factor:

<Eq
  id="complex-inner-product"
  label="Inner product on C-n"
  latex={String.raw`\langle u \vert v \rangle = \sum_{i} u_i^{*} v_i`}
/>

Now $\langle v \vert v \rangle = (i)^{*}(i) = (-i)(i) = 1$, which is real and positive. That
is the entire reason for the star.

<Derivation caption="An inner product in C-two">
  <Step
    why="Take u = (1, i) and v = (i, 1). Conjugate the components of u first."
    latex={String.raw`u^{*} = (1,\; -i)`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Multiply componentwise against v and add."
    latex={String.raw`\langle u \vert v \rangle = (1)(i) + (-i)(1)`}
  />
  <Step why="These cancel exactly." latex={String.raw`= i - i = 0`} />
  <Step
    why="Zero inner product means orthogonal — even though neither vector is real."
    latex={String.raw`\implies u \perp v`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Callout kind="warning">
Order matters now. $\langle u \vert v \rangle$ and $\langle v \vert u \rangle$ are
complex conjugates of each other, not equal. Physics also conjugates the **first** slot; most
maths textbooks conjugate the second. Both conventions are in print — check which one a paper is
using before trusting a sign.
</Callout>

### The axioms

<Axioms
  caption="An inner product is any operation satisfying these"
  items={[
    ['\\langle u \\vert v \\rangle = \\langle v \\vert u \\rangle^{*}', 'Conjugate symmetry'],
    ['\\langle u \\vert \\alpha v + \\beta w \\rangle = \\alpha\\langle u \\vert v \\rangle + \\beta\\langle u \\vert w \\rangle', 'Linear in the second slot'],
    ['\\langle \\alpha u \\vert v \\rangle = \\alpha^{*}\\langle u \\vert v \\rangle', 'Conjugate-linear in the first'],
    ['\\langle v \\vert v \\rangle \\geq 0', 'Positive'],
    ['\\langle v \\vert v \\rangle = 0 \\iff v = 0', 'Only the zero vector has zero length'],
  ]}
/>

## 4. Norm and distance

The inner product of a vector with itself gives its length, called the **norm**:

<Eq
  id="norm"
  label="Norm"
  latex={String.raw`\lVert v \rVert = \sqrt{\langle v \vert v \rangle}`}
/>

<Derivation caption="Normalising (3, 4)">
  <Step why="Compute the inner product with itself." latex={String.raw`\langle v \vert v \rangle = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25`} />
  <Step why="Take the square root." latex={String.raw`\lVert v \rVert = 5`} />
  <Step
    why="Divide by the norm. The result has length 1 and the same direction."
    latex={String.raw`\hat{v} = \tfrac{1}{5}(3, 4) = (0.6,\; 0.8)`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Definition term="Normalised">
A vector with $\lVert v \rVert = 1$. Every physical quantum state is normalised, because
$\lVert \psi \rVert^2$ is a total probability and must come to 1.
</Definition>

## 5. Orthogonality and orthonormal bases

<Definition term="Orthogonal">
Two vectors with $\langle u \vert v \rangle = 0$. Geometrically perpendicular; physically,
perfectly distinguishable states.
</Definition>

A basis whose vectors are all mutually orthogonal *and* all normalised is **orthonormal**. The
two conditions pack into one statement:

<Eq
  id="orthonormality"
  label="Orthonormality"
  latex={String.raw`\langle e_m \vert e_n \rangle = \delta_{mn} = \begin{cases} 1 & m = n \\ 0 & m \neq n \end{cases}`}
/>

This is the equation from your checklist's "key equations to master", and now you can read it:
*every basis vector has length one, and any two different ones are perpendicular.*

## 6. Why an orthonormal basis is worth so much

Expand a vector in a basis. In general, finding the coefficients means solving simultaneous
equations. With an orthonormal basis, each coefficient falls out of a single inner product.

<Derivation caption="Extracting a coefficient">
  <Step why="Write v in the basis, with unknown coefficients." latex={String.raw`v = \sum_n c_n e_n`} />
  <Step
    why="Take the inner product with one particular basis vector e_m."
    latex={String.raw`\langle e_m \vert v \rangle = \Big\langle e_m \Big\vert \sum_n c_n e_n \Big\rangle`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Linearity in the second slot lets the sum come out."
    latex={String.raw`= \sum_n c_n \langle e_m \vert e_n \rangle`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Orthonormality kills every term except n = m."
    latex={String.raw`= \sum_n c_n \delta_{mn} = c_m`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Eq
  id="coefficient"
  label="The coefficient is just a projection"
  latex={String.raw`c_n = \langle e_n \vert v \rangle`}
/>

<Callout kind="forward">
That green bar in the figure above *is* $c_n$. Projection and expansion coefficient are the
same object. When you later compute a scattering amplitude as $\langle \text{out} \vert \text{in} \rangle$, you are asking exactly this: how much of the incoming state lies along the
outgoing one.
</Callout>

Reassembling the vector from its coefficients gives the **completeness relation**:

<Eq
  id="completeness"
  label="Completeness"
  latex={String.raw`\sum_n \lvert e_n \rangle \langle e_n \rvert = \mathbb{1}`}
/>

Read it as an instruction: *project onto every basis direction, add the pieces back up, and you
recover what you started with.* Nothing is lost — the basis misses no direction.

## 7. The "Hilbert" part: completeness of the space

Everything so far works in finitely many dimensions. Quantum mechanics needs infinitely many, and
infinite dimensions bring one genuine hazard.

<Callout kind="note">
The rational numbers have holes. The sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, ... has entries that crowd
arbitrarily close together, yet its limit $\sqrt{2}$ is **not rational**. The sequence tries
to converge and the space has nowhere to put the answer.
</Callout>

A sequence whose terms eventually crowd arbitrarily close together is a **Cauchy sequence**. A
space is **complete** when every such sequence actually converges to a point *inside the space* —
no holes.

<Definition term="Hilbert space">
A vector space with an inner product that is also complete. Three ingredients: vectors, an inner
product, no holes.
</Definition>

<Compare
  caption="Building up to it"
  columns={['Structure', 'What it adds', 'Can you do physics in it?']}
  rows={[
    ['Vector space', 'add and scale', 'superposition only'],
    ['Inner product space', 'length, angle, overlap', 'probabilities, but infinite sums may escape'],
    ['Hilbert space', 'completeness', 'yes — this is the setting of quantum mechanics'],
  ]}
/>

Completeness matters because a quantum state is routinely an *infinite* sum
$\lvert\psi\rangle = \sum_n c_n \lvert e_n \rangle$. Without it, that sum could converge to
something that is not a state at all, and the theory would be talking about nothing.

## 8. The spaces you will actually use

<Compare
  columns={['Space', 'Elements', 'Dimension', 'Where it shows up']}
  rows={[
    ['$\\mathbb{C}^2$', 'pairs of complex numbers', '2', 'spin-½ — every neutron in this syllabus'],
    ['$\\mathbb{C}^n$', '$n$ complex numbers', '$n$', 'any finite set of levels'],
    ['$\\ell^2$', 'sequences with $\\sum |c_n|^2 < \\infty$', 'infinite', 'coefficients in a discrete basis'],
    ['$L^2(\\mathbb{R}^3)$', 'functions with $\\int |\\psi|^2 \\, d^3r < \\infty$', 'infinite', 'wavefunctions'],
  ]}
/>

$L^2$ is the important one. Its membership condition is exactly the normalisation
requirement: a function belongs only if its total probability is finite. The space of physically
sensible wavefunctions and the space $L^2$ are the same space — that is not a coincidence,
it is the definition doing its job.

For $L^2$ the inner product becomes an integral rather than a sum:

<Eq
  id="l2-inner-product"
  label="Inner product on L-two"
  latex={String.raw`\langle \phi \vert \psi \rangle = \int \phi^{*}(\mathbf{r})\, \psi(\mathbf{r}) \, d^3r`}
/>

Same operation: conjugate the first, multiply, add up. A sum over components becomes an integral
over positions because the index has become continuous.

## 9. One inequality worth knowing

<Eq
  id="cauchy-schwarz"
  label="Cauchy-Schwarz"
  latex={String.raw`\lvert \langle u \vert v \rangle \rvert \leq \lVert u \rVert \, \lVert v \rVert`}
/>

Overlap can never exceed the product of the lengths. For normalised states this says
$\lvert\langle \phi \vert \psi \rangle\rvert \leq 1$ — which is what makes
$\lvert\langle \phi \vert \psi \rangle\rvert^2$ usable as a probability. Equality holds only
when the two vectors are parallel, meaning they are the same state up to a phase.
