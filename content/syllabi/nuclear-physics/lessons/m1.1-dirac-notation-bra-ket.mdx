<Callout kind="why">
You have been reading this notation for a lesson already. Now it gets taken apart. The payoff is
that $\ket{\psi}\bra{\phi}$ — a ket times a bra, in that order — is an **operator**, and once you
see that, projection, completeness and changing basis all become the same small move rather than
three separate techniques.
</Callout>

## 1. Splitting the bracket

Dirac's observation was that the bracket $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$ has two halves worth naming.

<Compare
  columns={['Object', 'Written', 'What it is']}
  rows={[
    ['Ket', '$\\ket{\\psi}$', 'a state vector — a column, in any chosen basis'],
    ['Bra', '$\\bra{\\phi}$', 'its dual — a row, conjugated'],
    ['Bracket', '$\\braket{\\phi}{\\psi}$', 'a bra meeting a ket: a single complex number'],
  ]}
/>

The pun is deliberate: **bra** + **ket** = bracket.

<Definition term="Ket">
A vector in the Hilbert space. $\ket{\psi}$ names the *state*, without committing to a basis —
which is the notation's first real advantage.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Bra">
The dual of a ket: its conjugate transpose. Where $\ket{\psi}$ is a column, $\bra{\psi}$ is the
row you get by transposing and conjugating every entry.
</Definition>

## 2. In components

Take a state in $\mathbb{C}^2$ — a spin, say.

<Eq
  id="ket-components"
  label="A ket as a column"
  latex={String.raw`\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}`}
/>

<Eq
  id="bra-components"
  label="Its bra: transpose and conjugate"
  latex={String.raw`\bra{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha^{*} & \beta^{*} \end{pmatrix}`}
/>

Putting them together, row times column, gives a number:

<Eq
  id="bracket-components"
  label="The bracket is a number"
  latex={String.raw`\braket{\psi}{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha^{*} & \beta^{*} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} = |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2`}
/>

Which is the norm-squared from last lesson, now with the conjugation built into the notation
rather than remembered as a rule.

<Callout kind="note">
The conjugation lives in the **bra**. That is why $\braket{\phi}{\psi} = \braket{\psi}{\phi}^{*}$
falls out automatically: swapping which vector supplies the bra swaps which one gets conjugated.
</Callout>

## 3. The move that earns the notation: outer products

Row times column gives a number. **Column times row gives a matrix.**

<Eq
  id="outer-product"
  label="An outer product is an operator"
  latex={String.raw`\ket{\psi}\bra{\phi} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \gamma^{*} & \delta^{*} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha\gamma^{*} & \alpha\delta^{*} \\ \beta\gamma^{*} & \beta\delta^{*} \end{pmatrix}`}
/>

<Compare
  caption="Order is everything"
  columns={['Written', 'Shape', 'Result']}
  rows={[
    ['$\\braket{\\phi}{\\psi}$', 'row × column', 'a number (a scalar)'],
    ['$\\ket{\\psi}\\bra{\\phi}$', 'column × row', 'a matrix (an operator)'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
This is the one thing to get right. $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$ and $\ket{\psi}\bra{\phi}$ are built
from the same two states and are completely different kinds of object — a number you could put on
a graph, and a machine that eats a state and returns another state.
</Callout>

## 4. Projection operators

The most useful outer product puts a normalised state against itself:

<Eq
  id="projector"
  label="Projector onto a state"
  latex={String.raw`\hat{P}_e = \ket{e}\bra{e}`}
/>

Apply it to a state and watch what the bracketing does:

<Derivation caption="Why this projects">
  <Step
    why="Apply the operator. Nothing has been assumed except associativity."
    latex={String.raw`\hat{P}_e \ket{\psi} = \ket{e}\bra{e}\,\ket{\psi}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Regroup: the bra and ket in the middle form a bracket, which is just a number."
    latex={String.raw`= \ket{e} \braket{e}{\psi}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="A number times a ket. The result points along e, scaled by how much of psi lay along e."
    latex={String.raw`= \braket{e}{\psi} \, \ket{e}`}
  />
</Derivation>

That coefficient $\braket{e}{\psi}$ is the same projection you dragged around in the last lesson.
Notice the notation did the work: regrouping the symbols *was* the derivation.

## 5. Completeness, as an operator

Sum the projectors over a complete orthonormal basis and you get the identity:

<Eq
  id="completeness-operator"
  label="Completeness"
  latex={String.raw`\sum_n \ket{e_n}\bra{e_n} = \mathbb{1}`}
/>

Last lesson this was a statement about recovering a vector from its components. Here it is an
**equation between operators**, and that is what makes it a tool: anywhere you see a product of
brackets, you may insert $\mathbb{1}$ for free.

<Derivation caption="Inserting the identity — the workhorse move">
  <Step
    why="Start with an overlap you want to compute but cannot evaluate directly."
    latex={String.raw`\braket{\phi}{\psi}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Insert the identity between them. Legal, because the identity changes nothing."
    latex={String.raw`= \bra{\phi} \left( \sum_n \ket{e_n}\bra{e_n} \right) \ket{\psi}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Regroup into brackets. Each term is now two ordinary numbers multiplied."
    latex={String.raw`= \sum_n \braket{\phi}{e_n} \braket{e_n}{\psi}`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Callout kind="forward">
You will use that move constantly. It is how a position-space integral turns into a momentum-space
one, and how the sum over intermediate states appears in a scattering amplitude. In Hauser-Feshbach
theory the sum over channels is this identity inserted between an entrance and an exit state.
</Callout>

## 6. A state versus its representation

$\ket{\psi}$ is the state itself. To get numbers you must choose a basis and take brackets.

<Compare
  caption="One state, different coordinates"
  columns={['Expression', 'Meaning']}
  rows={[
    ['$\\ket{\\psi}$', 'the state — basis-free'],
    ['$\\braket{e_n}{\\psi}$', 'its component along $e_n$'],
    ['$\\braket{x}{\\psi} = \\psi(x)$', 'the wavefunction — its component in the position basis'],
    ['$\\braket{p}{\\psi} = \\tilde{\\psi}(p)$', 'the same state in the momentum basis'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="note">
The wavefunction is not the state. It is the state's shadow in one particular basis, exactly as
$(3, 2)$ is not the arrow but its components in one particular pair of axes. Changing basis
changes every number and leaves $\ket{\psi}$ untouched.
</Callout>

## 7. Operators and matrix elements

An operator maps kets to kets: $\hat{A}\ket{\psi} = \ket{\varphi}$. Sandwiching one between a bra
and a ket gives a **matrix element**:

<Eq
  id="matrix-element"
  label="Matrix element"
  latex={String.raw`A_{mn} = \braoket{e_m}{\hat{A}}{e_n}`}
/>

Collect those numbers over all $m, n$ and you have the matrix of the operator in that basis — the
bridge from abstract operator to something you can hand a computer.

<Eq
  id="expectation"
  label="Expectation value"
  latex={String.raw`\langle \hat{A} \rangle = \braoket{\psi}{\hat{A}}{\psi}`}
/>

This is the equation on your checklist as "expectation value", and now each piece has a name: a
bra, an operator, a ket, evaluating to the average you would measure over many identical systems.
