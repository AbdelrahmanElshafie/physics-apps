<Callout kind="why">
Everything so far has been basis-free, which is elegant and completely uncomputable. This lesson
is the bridge: pick a basis, and each operator collapses into a grid of numbers, each state into a
column. Two things fall out that are worth the trip — matrix multiplication turns out to be
*forced* rather than defined, and the same operator gets a different matrix in every basis.
</Callout>

## 1. The recipe

Sandwich the operator between two basis vectors:

<Eq
  id="matrix-element-def"
  label="The matrix element"
  latex={String.raw`A_{mn} = \braoket{e_m}{\hat{A}}{e_n}`}
/>

Run $m$ down the rows and $n$ across the columns and you have filled in a grid. For an
$N$-dimensional space that is $N^2$ numbers, and those numbers *are* the operator — as far as any
calculation is concerned.

Where does the recipe come from? Insert the identity twice.

<Derivation caption="Why those are the right numbers">
  <Step
    why="Start with the operator acting on a basis vector. The result is some ket."
    latex={String.raw`\hat{A}\ket{e_n}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Any ket can be expanded in the basis — insert the identity."
    latex={String.raw`= \left(\sum_m \ket{e_m}\bra{e_m}\right)\hat{A}\ket{e_n}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Regroup. The bracket is a number, so this is a weighted sum of basis kets."
    latex={String.raw`= \sum_m \braoket{e_m}{\hat{A}}{e_n} \, \ket{e_m}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Those weights are exactly the column of the matrix under index n."
    latex={String.raw`= \sum_m A_{mn} \ket{e_m}`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Callout kind="note">
Read that last line carefully: column $n$ of the matrix tells you **what $\hat{A}$ does to the
$n$-th basis vector**. That is the most useful way to hold a matrix in your head — not as a grid
of numbers, but as a list of where each basis direction goes.
</Callout>

## 2. States become columns

The same move on a state gives its components:

<Eq
  id="state-column"
  label="A state as a column"
  latex={String.raw`\psi_n = \braket{e_n}{\psi} \qquad \ket{\psi} \;\longrightarrow\; \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \psi_2 \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}`}
/>

And the abstract statement $\hat{A}\ket{\psi} = \ket{\varphi}$ becomes an ordinary matrix acting on
an ordinary column:

<Eq
  id="matrix-acting"
  label="The operator equation, in components"
  latex={String.raw`\varphi_m = \sum_n A_{mn} \psi_n`}
/>

## 3. Composition is forced to be matrix multiplication

Nobody decided that matrices multiply row-into-column. It follows from completeness.

<Derivation caption="Deriving matrix multiplication">
  <Step
    why="Take the matrix element of a product of two operators."
    latex={String.raw`(AB)_{mn} = \braoket{e_m}{\hat{A}\hat{B}}{e_n}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Insert the identity between them — the workhorse move again."
    latex={String.raw`= \bra{e_m}\hat{A}\left(\sum_k \ket{e_k}\bra{e_k}\right)\hat{B}\ket{e_n}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Regroup into two matrix elements."
    latex={String.raw`= \sum_k \braoket{e_m}{\hat{A}}{e_k} \braoket{e_k}{\hat{B}}{e_n}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="Which is precisely the rule you were taught to memorise."
    latex={String.raw`= \sum_k A_{mk} B_{kn}`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Callout kind="why">
This is worth pausing on. Matrix multiplication looks arbitrary when you first meet it — why *that*
combination of rows and columns? Because it is what composing two operators does, once you write
both in a basis. The rule is a theorem, not a convention.
</Callout>

The same argument turns the abstract dagger into the concrete conjugate transpose:

<Eq
  id="adjoint-components"
  label="The adjoint, in components"
  latex={String.raw`\left(A^{\dagger}\right)_{mn} = \left(A_{nm}\right)^{*}`}
/>

So a Hermitian operator is a matrix equal to its own conjugate transpose: real on the diagonal,
and mirrored-and-conjugated off it.

## 4. Worked example: one operator, two bases

Take the spin-flip operator on $\mathbb{C}^2$, in the standard basis
$\ket{0} = (1,0)$, $\ket{1} = (0,1)$:

<Eq
  id="sigma-x-standard"
  label="Spin flip in the standard basis"
  latex={String.raw`\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}`}
/>

Column 1 is $(0,1)$ — it sends $\ket{0}$ to $\ket{1}$, which is what "flip" means. Now change to
the basis of its own combinations:

<Eq
  id="plus-minus-basis"
  label="A different basis"
  latex={String.raw`\ket{+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} + \ket{1}\right), \qquad \ket{-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)`}
/>

<Derivation caption="Recomputing the matrix elements">
  <Step
    why="Apply the flip to the plus state: it swaps the two components and nothing changes."
    latex={String.raw`\sigma_x \ket{+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{1} + \ket{0}\right) = +\ket{+}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="On the minus state the swap reverses the sign."
    latex={String.raw`\sigma_x \ket{-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{1} - \ket{0}\right) = -\ket{-}`}
  />
  <Step
    why="So the diagonal elements are plus and minus one, and the off-diagonal ones vanish."
    latex={String.raw`\sigma_x' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}`}
  />
</Derivation>

<Compare
  caption="The same operator, written twice"
  columns={['Basis', 'Matrix', 'Reading']}
  rows={[
    ['$\\{\\ket{0}, \\ket{1}\\}$', 'off-diagonal', 'it mixes the basis states'],
    ['$\\{\\ket{+}, \\ket{-}\\}$', 'diagonal', 'it merely rescales them'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
Nothing was done to the operator. It is the same physical instruction in both lines — only the
axes changed. A matrix is not an operator; it is an operator *plus a choice of basis*.
</Callout>

## 5. Changing basis

Collect the new basis vectors as the columns of a matrix $U$. Then:

<Eq
  id="similarity"
  label="Similarity transformation"
  latex={String.raw`A' = U^{\dagger} A U`}
/>

Because the columns of $U$ are orthonormal, $U$ is unitary — which is the concrete reason the last
lesson called unitary operators "changes of basis". For the example above,
$U = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying it out
returns exactly the diagonal matrix.

## 6. What survives a change of basis

If the numbers all change, what is actually a property of the operator?

<Compare
  caption="Invariants"
  columns={['Quantity', 'Standard basis', 'Plus-minus basis', 'Invariant?']}
  rows={[
    ['Entries', '$0, 1, 1, 0$', '$1, 0, 0, -1$', 'no'],
    ['Trace $\\operatorname{tr} A$', '$0 + 0 = 0$', '$1 + (-1) = 0$', 'yes'],
    ['Determinant', '$-1$', '$-1$', 'yes'],
    ['Eigenvalues', '$\\pm 1$', '$\\pm 1$', 'yes'],
  ]}
/>

Trace and determinant are basis-independent, so they are honest facts about the operator rather
than artefacts of how you wrote it down. This is why they turn up everywhere in nuclear physics:
a cross-section cannot depend on which axes you chose.

<Callout kind="forward">
Notice what happened in the worked example. In one basis the matrix was diagonal, and in that
basis the operator's action is trivial — multiply each component by a number. Those numbers were
$\pm 1$, the eigenvalues.

it. For the optical model that is the difference between a coupled system of equations and a set
of independent ones.
</Callout>
